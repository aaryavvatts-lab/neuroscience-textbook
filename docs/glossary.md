## A

#### Acetylcholine

A neurotransmitter that activates skeletal muscles at the neuromuscular junction and also helps control attention, memory, and many automatic body functions.

**Example:** Myasthenia gravis disrupts acetylcholine signaling at muscles, causing weakness.

**See also:** [Neuromuscular Junction](#neuromuscular-junction)

*Taught in [Chapter 3](chapters/03-synapses-and-neurotransmitters/index.md).*

#### Action Potential

A brief, self-renewing electrical pulse in which the membrane potential rapidly rises and then falls, traveling along an axon to carry a signal.

**Example:** Each action potential lasts only about one to two thousandths of a second, so a neuron can fire many of them each second.

**See also:** [Threshold](#threshold), [Refractory Period](#refractory-period)

*Taught in [Chapter 2](chapters/02-action-potential/index.md).*

#### Addiction

A treatable, long-lasting condition in which a person keeps using a substance or doing a behavior despite harm, linked to changes in brain circuits for reward, motivation, and self-control.

**Example:** Medicines combined with counseling help many people with an opioid use disorder recover.

**See also:** [Reward Pathway](#reward-pathway), [Tolerance and Withdrawal](#tolerance-and-withdrawal)

*Taught in [Chapter 15](chapters/15-psychiatric-disorders-and-addiction/index.md).*

#### Adolescent Brain

The brain during the teenage years, when it is heavily remodeled through synaptic pruning, increasing myelination, and changes in reward and control circuits.

**Example:** The adolescent brain's high plasticity makes the teen years a strong time to learn new languages, instruments, and sports.

**See also:** [Prefrontal Maturation](#prefrontal-maturation), [Reward Sensitivity in Teens](#reward-sensitivity-in-teens)

*Taught in [Chapter 8](chapters/08-developing-teenage-brain/index.md).*

#### Agonist and Antagonist

An agonist is a substance that activates a receptor, copying the natural signal; an antagonist binds a receptor and blocks it without activating it.

**Example:** Morphine is an opioid agonist, and naloxone is an opioid antagonist that can reverse an overdose.

**See also:** [Receptor](#receptor), [Naloxone](#naloxone)

*Taught in [Chapter 3](chapters/03-synapses-and-neurotransmitters/index.md).*

#### All-or-None Principle

The property of an action potential that it either fires at full size or does not fire at all, no matter how far a stimulus goes past threshold.

**Example:** A gentle tap and a hard tap produce action potentials of the same height; the hard tap instead produces more of them per second.

**See also:** [Firing Rate Coding](#firing-rate-coding)

*Taught in [Chapter 2](chapters/02-action-potential/index.md).*

#### Alzheimer's Disease

A progressive neurodegenerative disease marked by amyloid plaques and tau tangles in the brain that usually begins with trouble forming new memories.

**Example:** An early sign may be asking the same question again and again within a short time.

**See also:** [Amyloid Plaque](#amyloid-plaque), [Tau Tangle](#tau-tangle), [Anti-Amyloid Antibody](#anti-amyloid-antibody)

*Taught in [Chapter 13](chapters/13-aging-and-neurodegeneration/index.md).*

#### Amnesia

A serious loss of memory caused by brain injury, disease, or psychological trauma, affecting either the formation of new memories (anterograde) or recall of earlier ones (retrograde).

**Example:** After a concussion, a player may not remember the play that caused the injury.

**See also:** [Patient H.M.](#patient-hm)

*Taught in [Chapter 9](chapters/09-learning-memory-plasticity/index.md).*

#### Amygdala

An almond-shaped cluster of neurons deep in each temporal lobe that detects threats and other emotionally important events and helps form emotional memories.

**Example:** The amygdala responds to a fearful face even when it is flashed too quickly for a person to notice consciously.

**See also:** [Fear Conditioning](#fear-conditioning)

*Taught in [Chapter 11](chapters/11-emotion-and-stress/index.md).*

#### Amyloid Plaque

A clump of the protein fragment amyloid-beta that builds up between neurons and is a hallmark of Alzheimer's disease.

**Example:** A special PET scan can show amyloid plaques in a living person's brain.

**See also:** [Tau Tangle](#tau-tangle)

*Taught in [Chapter 13](chapters/13-aging-and-neurodegeneration/index.md).*

#### Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS)

A progressive neurodegenerative disease that destroys both upper and lower motor neurons, causing increasing muscle weakness and eventually paralysis.

**Example:** Most cases of ALS are not inherited, but some are caused by changes in genes such as SOD1.

**See also:** [Upper vs Lower Motor Neuron](#upper-vs-lower-motor-neuron), [Antisense Oligonucleotide](#antisense-oligonucleotide)

*Taught in [Chapter 13](chapters/13-aging-and-neurodegeneration/index.md).*

#### Anatomical Directions

Standard words that describe where body or brain parts sit relative to one another, such as anterior (front), posterior (back), superior (top), inferior (bottom), medial (toward the middle), and lateral (toward the side).

**Example:** The frontal lobe is anterior to the parietal lobe.

**See also:** [Brain Planes and Sections](#brain-planes-and-sections)

*Taught in [Chapter 4](chapters/04-neuroanatomy/index.md).*

#### Animal Models

Animals such as mice, rats, fruit flies, zebrafish, or sea slugs that are studied to understand biological processes or diseases that also occur in humans.

**Example:** Mice carrying the human gene change that causes Huntington's disease are used to test possible new treatments.

**See also:** [Aplysia Model](#aplysia-model), [Research Ethics](#research-ethics)

*Taught in [Chapter 9](chapters/09-learning-memory-plasticity/index.md).*

#### Anti-Amyloid Antibody

A lab-made antibody medicine that binds amyloid-beta and helps the immune system clear amyloid plaques from the brain.

**Example:** Lecanemab (approved in 2023) and donanemab (approved in 2024) are FDA-approved for early Alzheimer's disease; they modestly slow decline and require MRI monitoring for brain swelling or bleeding.

**See also:** [Amyloid Plaque](#amyloid-plaque), [Biomarker](#biomarker)

*Taught in [Chapter 13](chapters/13-aging-and-neurodegeneration/index.md).*

#### Antisense Oligonucleotide

A short, lab-made strand of genetic material designed to stick to a specific RNA message and block or change how a protein is made from it.

**Example:** Tofersen, an antisense oligonucleotide that lowers SOD1 protein, received FDA accelerated approval in 2023 for ALS caused by SOD1 gene changes.

**See also:** [Gene Therapy](#gene-therapy), [Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS)](#amyotrophic-lateral-sclerosis-als)

*Taught in [Chapter 13](chapters/13-aging-and-neurodegeneration/index.md).*

#### Anxiety Disorders

A group of mental health conditions in which fear or worry is intense, long-lasting, and out of proportion to the situation, interfering with daily life.

**Example:** Generalized anxiety disorder, panic disorder, and social anxiety disorder are all anxiety disorders.

**See also:** [Amygdala](#amygdala)

*Taught in [Chapter 15](chapters/15-psychiatric-disorders-and-addiction/index.md).*

#### Aphasia

A language disorder caused by brain damage, often from a stroke, that affects speaking, understanding, reading, or writing, while general intelligence is often spared.

**Example:** A grandmother with aphasia after a stroke may know exactly what she wants to say but be unable to find the words.

**See also:** [Broca's Area](#brocas-area), [Wernicke's Area](#wernickes-area)

*Taught in [Chapter 12](chapters/12-language-attention-decisions/index.md).*

#### Aplysia Model

The use of the sea slug *Aplysia californica*, which has relatively few and unusually large neurons, to study how learning changes synapses.

**Example:** Eric Kandel used *Aplysia* to show that simple memories involve changes in synaptic strength, work recognized with a Nobel Prize in 2000.

**See also:** [Habituation and Sensitization](#habituation-and-sensitization), [Animal Models](#animal-models)

*Taught in [Chapter 9](chapters/09-learning-memory-plasticity/index.md).*

#### Astrocyte

A star-shaped glial cell that supplies neurons with nutrients, controls the chemical surroundings of synapses, and helps form the blood-brain barrier.

**Example:** After a brain injury, astrocytes multiply and form a scar that walls off the damaged area.

**See also:** [Blood-Brain Barrier](#blood-brain-barrier)

*Taught in [Chapter 1](chapters/01-neurons-and-glia/index.md).*

#### Attention

The brain's ability to select some information for focused processing while ignoring the rest.

**Example:** In a noisy cafeteria, attention lets you follow your friend's voice and tune out other conversations.

**See also:** [Executive Function](#executive-function)

*Taught in [Chapter 12](chapters/12-language-attention-decisions/index.md).*

#### Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)

A developmental condition marked by ongoing patterns of inattention, hyperactivity, or impulsivity that interfere with daily life.

**Example:** A student with ADHD might lose track of assignments and find it hard to stay seated, and may be helped by behavioral strategies, school supports, or medication.

**See also:** [Attention](#attention), [Executive Function](#executive-function)

*Taught in [Chapter 15](chapters/15-psychiatric-disorders-and-addiction/index.md).*

#### Auditory Pathway

The chain of connections that carries sound information from the cochlea through the brainstem and thalamus to the auditory cortex in the temporal lobe.

**Example:** Because the auditory pathway sends information from each ear to both sides of the brain, a stroke on one side rarely causes deafness in one ear.

**See also:** [Cochlea and Hair Cells](#cochlea-and-hair-cells)

*Taught in [Chapter 6](chapters/06-hearing-touch-taste-smell-pain/index.md).*

#### Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)

A developmental condition involving differences in social communication and interaction along with focused interests or repetitive behaviors, which vary widely from person to person.

**Example:** One autistic person may need daily support while another excels in a specialized career; both are on the autism spectrum.

**See also:** [Nature and Nurture](#nature-and-nurture)

*Taught in [Chapter 15](chapters/15-psychiatric-disorders-and-addiction/index.md).*

#### Autonomic Nervous System

The part of the nervous system that controls automatic body functions, such as heart rate, digestion, sweating, and pupil size, without conscious effort.

**Example:** You do not have to think about digesting lunch, because your autonomic nervous system handles it.

**See also:** [Sympathetic vs Parasympathetic](#sympathetic-vs-parasympathetic)

*Taught in [Chapter 4](chapters/04-neuroanatomy/index.md).*

#### Axon

A long, thin extension of a neuron that carries electrical signals away from the cell body toward other cells.

**Example:** A numbing shot at the dentist blocks signals traveling along the axons of pain-sensing neurons in your gums.

**See also:** [Dendrite](#dendrite), [Myelin Sheath](#myelin-sheath)

*Taught in [Chapter 1](chapters/01-neurons-and-glia/index.md).*

#### Axon Terminal

The swollen tip at the end of an axon branch, where a neuron releases chemical messengers onto another cell.

**Example:** Botulinum toxin (the active ingredient in Botox) acts at axon terminals, stopping them from releasing acetylcholine onto muscles.

**See also:** [Synaptic Vesicle](#synaptic-vesicle)

*Taught in [Chapter 1](chapters/01-neurons-and-glia/index.md).*

## B

#### Basal Ganglia

A group of structures deep in the brain that help select which movements to make and which to hold back, and that also take part in habits and reward.

**Example:** Parkinson's disease and Huntington's disease both disrupt the basal ganglia.

**See also:** [Direct and Indirect Pathways](#direct-and-indirect-pathways)

*Taught in [Chapter 7](chapters/07-movement/index.md).*

#### BE-FAST Warning Signs

A memory aid for spotting a possible stroke: Balance loss, Eyes (vision changes), Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, and Time to call emergency services.

**Example:** If a grandparent suddenly has slurred speech and a drooping smile, BE-FAST tells you to call 911 right away.

**See also:** [Stroke](#stroke)

*Taught in [Chapter 14](chapters/14-injury-stroke-epilepsy/index.md).*

#### Biomarker

A measurable sign in the body, such as a substance in blood or spinal fluid or a feature on a scan, that indicates a normal process, a disease, or a response to treatment.

**Example:** Neurofilament light, a protein released when nerve cells are damaged, can be measured in blood or spinal fluid as a biomarker.

**See also:** [PET Scan (Positron Emission Tomography)](#pet-scan-positron-emission-tomography), [Lumbar Puncture](#lumbar-puncture)

*Taught in [Chapter 13](chapters/13-aging-and-neurodegeneration/index.md).*

#### Blood-Brain Barrier

A tightly sealed lining of the brain's blood vessels that blocks many substances in the blood from entering brain tissue while letting needed nutrients through.

**Example:** Dopamine pills would not help Parkinson's disease because dopamine cannot cross the blood-brain barrier, so doctors give levodopa, which can.

**See also:** [Astrocyte](#astrocyte), [Levodopa](#levodopa)

*Taught in [Chapter 1](chapters/01-neurons-and-glia/index.md).*

#### Brain Planes and Sections

The standard ways of slicing the brain for viewing: sagittal (separating left from right), coronal (separating front from back), and horizontal or axial (separating top from bottom).

**Example:** CT scans of the head are usually viewed as a stack of axial slices.

**See also:** [Anatomical Directions](#anatomical-directions)

*Taught in [Chapter 4](chapters/04-neuroanatomy/index.md).*

#### Brain Waves

Rhythmic patterns of electrical activity produced by large groups of neurons firing together, described by their speed (frequency), such as delta, theta, alpha, beta, and gamma.

**Example:** Slow, large delta waves dominate deep NREM sleep.

**See also:** [Electroencephalogram (EEG)](#electroencephalogram-eeg)

*Taught in [Chapter 10](chapters/10-sleep-and-brain-states/index.md).*

#### Brain-Computer Interface (BCI)

A system that records brain activity and translates it into commands for an outside device, such as a computer cursor or robotic arm.

**Example:** In clinical trials, implanted brain-computer interfaces have let some people with paralysis type or move a cursor using brain signals alone. As of late 2026, no implanted brain-computer interface has FDA approval for long-term use, so these devices are still experimental.

**See also:** [Deep Brain Stimulation](#deep-brain-stimulation)

*Taught in [Chapter 16](chapters/16-how-doctors-examine-the-brain/index.md).*

#### Brainstem

The stalk of the brain that connects the cerebrum to the spinal cord, made of the midbrain, pons, and medulla, and controls breathing, heart rate, alertness, and many reflexes.

**Example:** Doctors check whether the pupils react to light to test brainstem function in an unresponsive patient.

**See also:** [Cranial Nerves](#cranial-nerves)

*Taught in [Chapter 4](chapters/04-neuroanatomy/index.md).*

#### Broca's Area

A region in the lower part of the frontal lobe, usually on the left, that is important for producing speech and building sentences.

**Example:** Damage to Broca's area can make speech slow, effortful, and missing small words like "the" and "is."

**See also:** [Wernicke's Area](#wernickes-area), [Aphasia](#aphasia)

*Taught in [Chapter 12](chapters/12-language-attention-decisions/index.md).*

## C

#### Cell Body (Soma)

The main part of a neuron that contains the nucleus and most of the machinery for making proteins and keeping the cell alive.

**Example:** In ALS, the cell bodies of motor neurons in the spinal cord die, so the muscles they control grow weak.

*Taught in [Chapter 1](chapters/01-neurons-and-glia/index.md).*

#### Cell Membrane

A thin, flexible double layer of fat molecules that surrounds every cell and controls which substances enter and leave it.

**Example:** A neuron's membrane keeps most sodium outside the cell, which sets up the electrical charge that neurons use to send signals.

**See also:** [Ion Channel](#ion-channel), [Concentration Gradient](#concentration-gradient)

*Taught in [Chapter 1](chapters/01-neurons-and-glia/index.md).*

#### Central Nervous System

The brain and spinal cord, which together process information and make decisions for the body.

**Example:** Multiple sclerosis is called a central nervous system disease because its damage is in the brain, spinal cord, and optic nerves.

**See also:** [Peripheral Nervous System](#peripheral-nervous-system)

*Taught in [Chapter 1](chapters/01-neurons-and-glia/index.md).*

#### Cerebellar Coordination

The cerebellum's job of comparing intended movements with actual movements and correcting their timing, force, and accuracy.

**Example:** A person with cerebellar damage may overshoot when reaching for a glass of water.

**See also:** [Cerebellum](#cerebellum), [Motor Learning](#motor-learning)

*Taught in [Chapter 7](chapters/07-movement/index.md).*

#### Cerebellum

A structure at the back of the brain, beneath the occipital lobes, that coordinates the timing and accuracy of movement, supports balance, and helps learn motor skills.

**Example:** Alcohol impairs the cerebellum, which is why roadside sobriety tests ask a person to walk in a straight line.

**See also:** [Cerebellar Coordination](#cerebellar-coordination)

*Taught in [Chapter 4](chapters/04-neuroanatomy/index.md).*

#### Cerebral Blood Supply

The network of arteries that delivers oxygen and glucose to the brain, coming mainly from two carotid arteries and two vertebral arteries that join in a ring at the base of the brain.

**Example:** A blockage in the middle cerebral artery, a common stroke site, can weaken the face and arm on the opposite side of the body.

**See also:** [Stroke](#stroke)

*Taught in [Chapter 4](chapters/04-neuroanatomy/index.md).*

#### Cerebral Cortex

The thin, folded outer layer of gray matter covering the two cerebral hemispheres, responsible for perception, voluntary movement, language, and thinking.

**Example:** The folds of the cortex pack a large surface area into the limited space of the skull.

**See also:** [Frontal Lobe](#frontal-lobe), [Localization of Function](#localization-of-function)

*Taught in [Chapter 4](chapters/04-neuroanatomy/index.md).*

#### Chemical Synapse

A synapse where the sending cell releases neurotransmitter molecules across a tiny gap to bind receptors on the receiving cell.

**Example:** Most synapses in the human brain are chemical synapses, which is why so many medicines and drugs act on them.

**See also:** [Electrical Synapse](#electrical-synapse)

*Taught in [Chapter 3](chapters/03-synapses-and-neurotransmitters/index.md).*

#### Chronic Stress Effects

The harmful changes that can result when the stress response stays switched on for weeks or months, affecting sleep, mood, memory, blood pressure, and immune function.

**Example:** In animal studies, long-lasting stress shrinks dendrites in the hippocampus.

**See also:** [Cortisol](#cortisol)

*Taught in [Chapter 11](chapters/11-emotion-and-stress/index.md).*

#### Chronic Traumatic Encephalopathy (CTE)

A progressive brain disease linked to repeated head impacts, identified by a specific pattern of tau buildup in the brain that can currently be confirmed only after death.

**Example:** CTE has been found in the brains of some former football players and other contact-sport athletes.

**See also:** [Tau Tangle](#tau-tangle), [Concussion](#concussion)

*Taught in [Chapter 14](chapters/14-injury-stroke-epilepsy/index.md).*

#### Circadian Rhythm

A roughly 24-hour cycle in body functions such as sleepiness, body temperature, and hormone levels, driven by an internal clock and reset by light.

**Example:** Jet lag happens when your circadian rhythm is out of step with the local time.

**See also:** [Suprachiasmatic Nucleus](#suprachiasmatic-nucleus), [Melatonin](#melatonin)

*Taught in [Chapter 10](chapters/10-sleep-and-brain-states/index.md).*

#### Clot-Busting Treatment

Medicine given through a vein to dissolve a blood clot that is causing an ischemic stroke, such as alteplase or tenecteplase, which helps most when given within a few hours of symptom onset.

**Example:** Because "time is brain," stroke teams track how many minutes pass between a patient's arrival and the start of clot-busting treatment.

**See also:** [Ischemic Stroke](#ischemic-stroke), [Computed Tomography (CT)](#computed-tomography-ct)

*Taught in [Chapter 14](chapters/14-injury-stroke-epilepsy/index.md).*

#### Cochlea and Hair Cells

The cochlea is the snail-shaped, fluid-filled part of the inner ear that turns sound vibrations into nerve signals; hair cells are its sensory cells, whose tiny bundles bend when the fluid vibrates.

**Example:** Loud concerts and earbuds at high volume can permanently damage hair cells and cause hearing loss.

**See also:** [Auditory Pathway](#auditory-pathway)

*Taught in [Chapter 6](chapters/06-hearing-touch-taste-smell-pain/index.md).*

#### Cognitive Reserve

The brain's ability to keep working well despite damage or disease, thought to be built through education, mentally challenging activities, and social connection.

**Example:** Two people with similar Alzheimer's changes on a brain scan may have very different symptoms, partly because of differences in cognitive reserve.

**See also:** [Normal Brain Aging](#normal-brain-aging)

*Taught in [Chapter 13](chapters/13-aging-and-neurodegeneration/index.md).*

#### Color Vision

The ability to tell apart different wavelengths of light, produced by comparing signals from three types of cones tuned to short, medium, and long wavelengths.

**Example:** In the most common form of color blindness, a person has trouble telling red from green because one type of cone is missing or altered.

**See also:** [Rods and Cones](#rods-and-cones)

*Taught in [Chapter 5](chapters/05-vision/index.md).*

#### Computed Tomography (CT)

An imaging test that combines many X-ray images taken from different angles into cross-sectional pictures of the body or brain.

**Example:** A CT scan is usually the first test for a suspected stroke because it quickly shows whether there is bleeding.

**See also:** [Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)](#magnetic-resonance-imaging-mri)

*Taught in [Chapter 16](chapters/16-how-doctors-examine-the-brain/index.md).*

#### Concentration Gradient

A difference in how much of a substance is present in one area compared with a nearby area, which causes the substance to drift from the crowded side toward the less crowded side.

**Example:** Potassium is much more concentrated inside a neuron than outside, so potassium tends to flow out whenever its channels open.

**See also:** [Sodium-Potassium Pump](#sodium-potassium-pump)

*Taught in [Chapter 1](chapters/01-neurons-and-glia/index.md).*

#### Concussion

A mild traumatic brain injury in which a blow or jolt makes the brain move rapidly inside the skull, temporarily disrupting how it works.

**Example:** A soccer player who feels dizzy and confused after a head-to-head collision may have a concussion and should be taken out of play and checked.

**See also:** [Return-to-Learn and Play](#return-to-learn-and-play), [Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI)](#traumatic-brain-injury-tbi)

*Taught in [Chapter 14](chapters/14-injury-stroke-epilepsy/index.md).*

#### Conduction Velocity

The speed at which an action potential travels along an axon, which increases with myelin and with a wider axon.

**Example:** Doctors use nerve conduction studies, which measure conduction velocity, to find nerves damaged by disease.

**See also:** [Saltatory Conduction](#saltatory-conduction)

*Taught in [Chapter 2](chapters/02-action-potential/index.md).*

#### Consciousness

The state of being awake and aware of yourself and your surroundings.

**Example:** In the emergency room, doctors judge a patient's level of consciousness by whether the patient opens their eyes, speaks, and follows commands.

**See also:** [Brainstem](#brainstem)

*Taught in [Chapter 10](chapters/10-sleep-and-brain-states/index.md).*

#### Corpus Callosum

A thick band of white matter that connects the left and right cerebral hemispheres and lets them share information.

**Example:** In split-brain surgery, the corpus callosum is cut to stop severe seizures from spreading from one hemisphere to the other.

**See also:** [Split-Brain Studies](#split-brain-studies)

*Taught in [Chapter 4](chapters/04-neuroanatomy/index.md).*

#### Correlation vs Causation

The distinction between two things that tend to occur together (correlation) and one thing actually producing the other (causation).

**Example:** Teens who sleep less may get lower grades, but that pattern alone does not prove that short sleep causes the lower grades.

**See also:** [Scientific Method in Neuroscience](#scientific-method-in-neuroscience)

*Taught in [Chapter 16](chapters/16-how-doctors-examine-the-brain/index.md).*

#### Corticospinal Tract

The main pathway carrying voluntary movement commands from the motor cortex down to the spinal cord, with most fibers crossing to the opposite side in the lower brainstem.

**Example:** Because the corticospinal tract crosses, damage to the right side of the brain weakens the left side of the body.

**See also:** [Primary Motor Cortex](#primary-motor-cortex)

*Taught in [Chapter 7](chapters/07-movement/index.md).*

#### Cortisol

A steroid hormone released by the adrenal glands that raises blood sugar, adjusts immune activity, and helps the body cope with stress.

**Example:** Cortisol levels naturally peak shortly after a person wakes up in the morning.

**See also:** [HPA Axis (Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal Axis)](#hpa-axis-hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal-axis)

*Taught in [Chapter 11](chapters/11-emotion-and-stress/index.md).*

#### Cranial Nerve Exam

A set of quick bedside tests of the twelve cranial nerves, such as checking pupil responses, eye movements, facial movement, hearing, and tongue movement.

**Example:** Asking a patient to smile and raise their eyebrows tests the facial nerve.

**See also:** [Cranial Nerves](#cranial-nerves)

*Taught in [Chapter 16](chapters/16-how-doctors-examine-the-brain/index.md).*

#### Cranial Nerves

Twelve pairs of nerves that leave directly from the brain and brainstem and mainly serve the head and neck, carrying smell, vision, eye and face movement, hearing, swallowing, and more.

**Example:** The seventh cranial nerve (the facial nerve) controls facial expression, so damage to it can make one side of the face droop.

**See also:** [Cranial Nerve Exam](#cranial-nerve-exam)

*Taught in [Chapter 4](chapters/04-neuroanatomy/index.md).*

#### Critical Period

A window in development when the brain is especially sensitive to certain experiences, which are needed for some abilities to develop normally.

**Example:** If a baby's cloudy lens (cataract) is not treated early, that eye may have lasting vision problems even after later surgery.

**See also:** [Neuroplasticity](#neuroplasticity)

*Taught in [Chapter 8](chapters/08-developing-teenage-brain/index.md).*

## D

#### Decision-Making

The process of choosing among options by weighing their likely outcomes, risks, and value.

**Example:** Choosing whether to study tonight or go to a movie involves decision-making circuits in the prefrontal cortex.

**See also:** [Reward Prediction Error](#reward-prediction-error)

*Taught in [Chapter 12](chapters/12-language-attention-decisions/index.md).*

#### Declarative Memory

Memory for facts and events that a person can consciously recall and put into words.

**Example:** Knowing that Paris is the capital of France and remembering your last birthday party are declarative memories.

**See also:** [Procedural Memory](#procedural-memory), [Hippocampus](#hippocampus)

*Taught in [Chapter 9](chapters/09-learning-memory-plasticity/index.md).*

#### Deep Brain Stimulation

A treatment in which thin electrodes are placed in specific deep brain areas and connected to a pacemaker-like device that sends steady electrical pulses.

**Example:** Deep brain stimulation can reduce tremor and stiffness in some people with Parkinson's disease when medicines alone are not enough.

**See also:** [Parkinson's Disease](#parkinsons-disease)

*Taught in [Chapter 7](chapters/07-movement/index.md).*

#### Dementia

A decline in memory, thinking, or other mental abilities severe enough to interfere with daily life, which can be caused by several different brain diseases.

**Example:** Alzheimer's disease is the most common cause of dementia.

**See also:** [Alzheimer's Disease](#alzheimers-disease), [Normal Brain Aging](#normal-brain-aging)

*Taught in [Chapter 13](chapters/13-aging-and-neurodegeneration/index.md).*

#### Dendrite

A branching extension of a neuron that receives signals from other cells and carries them toward the cell body.

**Example:** A Purkinje cell in the cerebellum has a huge, fan-shaped tree of dendrites that collects input from many thousands of other neurons.

**See also:** [Axon](#axon)

*Taught in [Chapter 1](chapters/01-neurons-and-glia/index.md).*

#### Depolarization

A change in membrane potential that makes the inside of a cell less negative, moving it toward zero or above.

**Example:** When positively charged sodium ions rush into a neuron, the cell depolarizes.

**See also:** [Repolarization](#repolarization), [Hyperpolarization](#hyperpolarization)

*Taught in [Chapter 2](chapters/02-action-potential/index.md).*

#### Depression

A common mood disorder involving persistent sadness or loss of interest in activities for at least two weeks, often with changes in sleep, appetite, energy, and concentration.

**Example:** A teen with depression might stop enjoying a favorite sport, sleep much more than usual, and find it hard to focus in class.

**See also:** [Serotonin](#serotonin), [Psychiatric Medication](#psychiatric-medication)

*Taught in [Chapter 15](chapters/15-psychiatric-disorders-and-addiction/index.md).*

#### Dermatome

An area of skin whose sensation is supplied by a single spinal nerve root.

**Example:** Shingles causes a painful rash in a band that follows one dermatome.

**See also:** [Somatosensory System](#somatosensory-system)

*Taught in [Chapter 6](chapters/06-hearing-touch-taste-smell-pain/index.md).*

#### Differential Diagnosis

A list of possible conditions that could explain a patient's symptoms, narrowed down using the history, exam, and tests.

**Example:** For sudden weakness on one side of the body, the differential diagnosis includes stroke, weakness after a seizure, and very low blood sugar.

**See also:** [Lesion Localization](#lesion-localization)

*Taught in [Chapter 16](chapters/16-how-doctors-examine-the-brain/index.md).*

#### Direct and Indirect Pathways

Two circuits through the basal ganglia: the direct pathway helps release wanted movements, and the indirect pathway helps hold back unwanted ones.

**Example:** Loss of dopamine in Parkinson's disease weakens the direct pathway and strengthens the indirect pathway, so movements become slow and hard to start.

**See also:** [Basal Ganglia](#basal-ganglia)

*Taught in [Chapter 7](chapters/07-movement/index.md).*

#### Dopamine

A neurotransmitter involved in movement, motivation, and learning from rewards.

**Example:** Parkinson's disease happens when dopamine-making neurons in the substantia nigra die.

**See also:** [Reward Pathway](#reward-pathway), [Substantia Nigra](#substantia-nigra)

*Taught in [Chapter 3](chapters/03-synapses-and-neurotransmitters/index.md).*

## E

#### Electrical Synapse

A synapse where two cells are directly linked by protein channels called gap junctions, letting electrical current pass straight from one cell to the next.

**Example:** Electrical synapses help groups of neurons in the brainstem fire in sync, as in circuits that control breathing rhythms.

**See also:** [Chemical Synapse](#chemical-synapse)

*Taught in [Chapter 3](chapters/03-synapses-and-neurotransmitters/index.md).*

#### Electroencephalogram (EEG)

A test that records the brain's electrical activity through small electrodes placed on the scalp.

**Example:** An EEG can show the spike patterns that help doctors diagnose epilepsy.

**See also:** [Brain Waves](#brain-waves)

*Taught in [Chapter 10](chapters/10-sleep-and-brain-states/index.md).*

#### Emotion

A brief, coordinated response to a meaningful event that includes feelings, body changes, and urges to act.

**Example:** The racing heart, sweaty palms, and urge to step back when you see a snake are all parts of an emotion.

**See also:** [Limbic System](#limbic-system), [Amygdala](#amygdala)

*Taught in [Chapter 11](chapters/11-emotion-and-stress/index.md).*

#### Emotion Regulation

The ways people influence which emotions they have and how strongly they feel and express them.

**Example:** Viewing a bad test grade as a clue about how to study better, rather than as proof of failure, is a form of emotion regulation.

**See also:** [Prefrontal Cortex](#prefrontal-cortex)

*Taught in [Chapter 11](chapters/11-emotion-and-stress/index.md).*

#### Epilepsy

A brain condition in which a person has a lasting tendency to have repeated, unprovoked seizures.

**Example:** Many people with epilepsy control their seizures well with daily medication and live full, active lives.

**See also:** [Seizure](#seizure), [Electroencephalogram (EEG)](#electroencephalogram-eeg)

*Taught in [Chapter 14](chapters/14-injury-stroke-epilepsy/index.md).*

#### Excitatory Postsynaptic Potential (EPSP)

A small, temporary depolarization in a receiving neuron that makes it more likely to fire an action potential.

**Example:** Glutamate binding to receptors on a dendrite usually produces an EPSP.

**See also:** [Inhibitory Postsynaptic Potential (IPSP)](#inhibitory-postsynaptic-potential-ipsp), [Synaptic Integration](#synaptic-integration)

*Taught in [Chapter 3](chapters/03-synapses-and-neurotransmitters/index.md).*

#### Executive Function

A set of mental skills, including planning, working memory, flexible thinking, and self-control, that help a person manage goals and behavior.

**Example:** Breaking a big science project into smaller tasks with deadlines uses executive function.

**See also:** [Prefrontal Cortex](#prefrontal-cortex), [Working Memory](#working-memory)

*Taught in [Chapter 12](chapters/12-language-attention-decisions/index.md).*

#### Eye and Retina

The eye is the organ that focuses light; the retina is the thin layer of neural tissue lining the back of the eye that detects light and begins processing images.

**Example:** Eye doctors look at the retina through the pupil to spot damage caused by diabetes or high blood pressure.

**See also:** [Photoreceptor](#photoreceptor)

*Taught in [Chapter 5](chapters/05-vision/index.md).*

## F

#### Fear Conditioning

A form of learning in which a neutral cue becomes linked with something unpleasant, so the cue alone comes to trigger fear.

**Example:** After being bitten by a dog, a person might feel anxious just hearing a bark.

**See also:** [Amygdala](#amygdala), [Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD)](#post-traumatic-stress-disorder-ptsd)

*Taught in [Chapter 11](chapters/11-emotion-and-stress/index.md).*

#### Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorders (FASD)

A range of lifelong physical, learning, and behavioral differences that can occur in a person who was exposed to alcohol before birth.

**Example:** A child with FASD may have trouble with attention, memory, and math, and benefits from early support at school.

**See also:** [Teratogen](#teratogen)

*Taught in [Chapter 8](chapters/08-developing-teenage-brain/index.md).*

#### Fight or Flight

The rapid, automatic reaction to danger in which sympathetic nerves and the hormone adrenaline speed the heart, open the airways, and send more blood to the muscles.

**Example:** Jumping back when a car suddenly swerves toward you is a fight-or-flight reaction.

**See also:** [Sympathetic vs Parasympathetic](#sympathetic-vs-parasympathetic), [Stress Response](#stress-response)

*Taught in [Chapter 11](chapters/11-emotion-and-stress/index.md).*

#### Firing Rate Coding

A way neurons represent the strength of a stimulus by how many action potentials they fire per second.

**Example:** A light touch might make a skin sensor fire a few times per second, while firm pressure makes it fire much faster.

**See also:** [All-or-None Principle](#all-or-none-principle)

*Taught in [Chapter 2](chapters/02-action-potential/index.md).*

#### Frontal Lobe

The front region of each cerebral hemisphere, involved in planning, decision-making, personality, voluntary movement, and speech production.

**Example:** Phineas Gage's personality changed after an iron rod damaged his frontal lobe in 1848.

**See also:** [Prefrontal Cortex](#prefrontal-cortex)

*Taught in [Chapter 4](chapters/04-neuroanatomy/index.md).*

#### Functional MRI (fMRI)

An MRI technique that tracks changes in blood oxygen levels to estimate which brain areas are more active during a task.

**Example:** Researchers use fMRI to see which brain regions become more active when people look at faces.

**See also:** [Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)](#magnetic-resonance-imaging-mri), [Correlation vs Causation](#correlation-vs-causation)

*Taught in [Chapter 16](chapters/16-how-doctors-examine-the-brain/index.md).*

## G

#### GABA (Gamma-Aminobutyric Acid)

The main inhibitory neurotransmitter of the brain, which makes receiving neurons less likely to fire.

**Example:** Benzodiazepine medicines, used for some seizures and anxiety, work by boosting the effect of GABA.

**See also:** [Glutamate](#glutamate)

*Taught in [Chapter 3](chapters/03-synapses-and-neurotransmitters/index.md).*

#### Gate Control Theory

The idea that nerve circuits in the spinal cord can "close a gate" on pain signals when non-painful touch signals arrive at the same time.

**Example:** Rubbing a bumped elbow makes it hurt less, as gate control theory predicts.

**See also:** [Pain Pathway](#pain-pathway)

*Taught in [Chapter 6](chapters/06-hearing-touch-taste-smell-pain/index.md).*

#### Gene Therapy

A treatment that adds, replaces, edits, or silences genes in a patient's cells to treat or prevent disease.

**Example:** Onasemnogene abeparvovec (Zolgensma), FDA-approved in 2019, delivers a working copy of the SMN1 gene to young children with spinal muscular atrophy.

**See also:** [Antisense Oligonucleotide](#antisense-oligonucleotide)

*Taught in [Chapter 13](chapters/13-aging-and-neurodegeneration/index.md).*

#### Glial Cell

A cell of the nervous system that is not a neuron and that supports, protects, insulates, or cleans up after neurons.

**Example:** Gliomas, a common type of tumor that starts in the brain, grow from glial cells rather than from neurons.

**See also:** [Astrocyte](#astrocyte), [Microglia](#microglia)

*Taught in [Chapter 1](chapters/01-neurons-and-glia/index.md).*

#### Glutamate

The main excitatory neurotransmitter of the brain, which makes receiving neurons more likely to fire.

**Example:** After a stroke, too much glutamate can overexcite and damage nearby neurons.

**See also:** [GABA (Gamma-Aminobutyric Acid)](#gaba-gamma-aminobutyric-acid)

*Taught in [Chapter 3](chapters/03-synapses-and-neurotransmitters/index.md).*

#### Gray Matter

Brain and spinal cord tissue made mostly of neuron cell bodies, dendrites, and synapses, where most information processing happens.

**Example:** The cerebral cortex is a thin outer layer of gray matter.

**See also:** [White Matter](#white-matter)

*Taught in [Chapter 4](chapters/04-neuroanatomy/index.md).*

#### Gustation

The sense of taste, in which taste receptor cells on the tongue detect sweet, salty, sour, bitter, and savory (umami) chemicals.

**Example:** Food seems bland when you have a cold because much of what we call "flavor" actually comes from smell.

**See also:** [Olfaction](#olfaction)

*Taught in [Chapter 6](chapters/06-hearing-touch-taste-smell-pain/index.md).*

## H

#### Habituation and Sensitization

Habituation is a shrinking response to a repeated, harmless stimulus; sensitization is a stronger response to many stimuli after a strong or harmful one.

**Example:** You stop noticing the hum of a refrigerator (habituation), but after a loud crash you jump at every small sound (sensitization).

**See also:** [Aplysia Model](#aplysia-model)

*Taught in [Chapter 9](chapters/09-learning-memory-plasticity/index.md).*

#### Hebbian Learning

The principle that when one neuron repeatedly helps make another neuron fire, the connection between them grows stronger, often summarized as "cells that fire together wire together."

**Example:** Repeatedly hearing a new friend's name while seeing her face links the two in your memory.

**See also:** [Long-Term Potentiation](#long-term-potentiation)

*Taught in [Chapter 9](chapters/09-learning-memory-plasticity/index.md).*

#### Hemispheric Lateralization

The tendency for some brain functions to depend more on one side (hemisphere) of the brain than on the other.

**Example:** Language is usually handled mostly by the left hemisphere, while recognizing faces relies more on the right.

**See also:** [Split-Brain Studies](#split-brain-studies)

*Taught in [Chapter 12](chapters/12-language-attention-decisions/index.md).*

#### Hemorrhagic Stroke

A stroke caused by a blood vessel bursting and bleeding into or around the brain.

**Example:** A sudden, extremely severe headache can be a warning sign of a hemorrhagic stroke.

**See also:** [Ischemic Stroke](#ischemic-stroke)

*Taught in [Chapter 14](chapters/14-injury-stroke-epilepsy/index.md).*

#### Hippocampus

A curved structure inside each temporal lobe that is essential for forming new memories of facts and events and for finding your way through space.

**Example:** Patient H.M. could not form new memories of facts and events after surgery removed much of the hippocampus on both sides.

**See also:** [Patient H.M.](#patient-hm), [Memory Consolidation](#memory-consolidation)

*Taught in [Chapter 9](chapters/09-learning-memory-plasticity/index.md).*

#### Homunculus

A map of the body laid out across the somatosensory or motor cortex, often drawn as a distorted figure whose body parts are sized by how much cortex they use.

**Example:** The homunculus has huge hands and lips because these body parts have many receptors and fine control.

**See also:** [Somatosensory Cortex](#somatosensory-cortex), [Primary Motor Cortex](#primary-motor-cortex)

*Taught in [Chapter 6](chapters/06-hearing-touch-taste-smell-pain/index.md).*

#### HPA Axis (Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal Axis)

A hormone chain in which the hypothalamus signals the pituitary gland, which in turn signals the adrenal glands to release cortisol during stress.

**Example:** Rising cortisol signals back to the hypothalamus and pituitary to turn the HPA axis down, like a thermostat.

**See also:** [Cortisol](#cortisol)

*Taught in [Chapter 11](chapters/11-emotion-and-stress/index.md).*

#### Huntington's Disease

An inherited neurodegenerative disease caused by an expanded, repeated stretch of DNA in the HTT gene, leading to uncontrolled movements, mood changes, and declining thinking skills, usually starting in adulthood.

**Example:** Each child of a parent with Huntington's disease has a 50 percent chance of inheriting the disease-causing gene.

**See also:** [Basal Ganglia](#basal-ganglia)

*Taught in [Chapter 13](chapters/13-aging-and-neurodegeneration/index.md).*

#### Hyperpolarization

A change in membrane potential that makes the inside of a cell more negative than its usual resting level.

**Example:** The neurotransmitter GABA often hyperpolarizes neurons, making them less likely to fire.

**See also:** [Depolarization](#depolarization)

*Taught in [Chapter 2](chapters/02-action-potential/index.md).*

#### Hypothalamus

A small region below the thalamus that controls hunger, thirst, body temperature, daily sleep cycles, and hormone release by the pituitary gland.

**Example:** During a fever, the hypothalamus raises the body's temperature set point.

**See also:** [HPA Axis (Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal Axis)](#hpa-axis-hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal-axis)

*Taught in [Chapter 4](chapters/04-neuroanatomy/index.md).*

## I

#### Inhibitory Postsynaptic Potential (IPSP)

A small, temporary change in a receiving neuron, usually a hyperpolarization, that makes it less likely to fire an action potential.

**Example:** GABA released by an inhibitory interneuron typically produces an IPSP.

**See also:** [Excitatory Postsynaptic Potential (EPSP)](#excitatory-postsynaptic-potential-epsp), [Synaptic Integration](#synaptic-integration)

*Taught in [Chapter 3](chapters/03-synapses-and-neurotransmitters/index.md).*

#### Interneuron

A neuron that connects other neurons within the brain or spinal cord, passing and shaping signals between incoming and outgoing pathways.

**Example:** During a knee-jerk reflex, spinal interneurons relax the hamstring muscle so the thigh muscle can straighten the leg.

**See also:** [Sensory Neuron](#sensory-neuron), [Motor Neuron](#motor-neuron)

*Taught in [Chapter 1](chapters/01-neurons-and-glia/index.md).*

#### Ion

An atom or small molecule that carries an electrical charge because it has gained or lost one or more electrons.

**Example:** Sodium (Na⁺), potassium (K⁺), calcium (Ca²⁺), and chloride (Cl⁻) are the main ions that let neurons fire.

**See also:** [Ion Channel](#ion-channel)

*Taught in [Chapter 1](chapters/01-neurons-and-glia/index.md).*

#### Ion Channel

A protein that forms a pore through the cell membrane, letting particular ions flow in or out when it is open.

**Example:** Many anticonvulsant medicines work by changing how sodium or calcium ion channels open in overactive neurons.

**See also:** [Voltage-Gated Channel](#voltage-gated-channel)

*Taught in [Chapter 2](chapters/02-action-potential/index.md).*

#### Ionotropic Receptor

A receptor that is itself an ion channel, opening directly when its neurotransmitter binds and producing a fast response.

**Example:** The acetylcholine receptors on skeletal muscle are ionotropic receptors.

**See also:** [Metabotropic Receptor](#metabotropic-receptor)

*Taught in [Chapter 3](chapters/03-synapses-and-neurotransmitters/index.md).*

#### Ischemic Stroke

A stroke caused by a blocked artery that cuts off blood flow to part of the brain.

**Example:** Most strokes are ischemic, often caused by a blood clot that travels from the heart or forms in a narrowed artery.

**See also:** [Clot-Busting Treatment](#clot-busting-treatment)

*Taught in [Chapter 14](chapters/14-injury-stroke-epilepsy/index.md).*

## L

#### Language Areas

The brain regions, usually in the left hemisphere, that support understanding and producing language.

**Example:** In most right-handed people, the language areas are in the left hemisphere.

**See also:** [Broca's Area](#brocas-area), [Wernicke's Area](#wernickes-area)

*Taught in [Chapter 12](chapters/12-language-attention-decisions/index.md).*

#### Learning

The process by which experience produces a lasting change in knowledge or behavior.

**Example:** After touching a cactus once, a young child avoids touching it again.

**See also:** [Memory](#memory), [Neuroplasticity](#neuroplasticity)

*Taught in [Chapter 9](chapters/09-learning-memory-plasticity/index.md).*

#### Lesion Localization

The reasoning doctors use to work out where in the nervous system an area of damage (a lesion) is, based on the pattern of symptoms and exam findings.

**Example:** Weakness of the right face, arm, and leg together with trouble speaking localizes the lesion to the left side of the brain.

**See also:** [Localization of Function](#localization-of-function), [Differential Diagnosis](#differential-diagnosis)

*Taught in [Chapter 16](chapters/16-how-doctors-examine-the-brain/index.md).*

#### Levodopa

A medicine for Parkinson's disease that crosses the blood-brain barrier and is converted into dopamine inside the brain.

**Example:** Levodopa is usually combined with carbidopa, which keeps it from turning into dopamine before it reaches the brain.

**See also:** [Parkinson's Disease](#parkinsons-disease)

*Taught in [Chapter 7](chapters/07-movement/index.md).*

#### Limbic System

A loosely defined group of connected brain structures, including the amygdala, hippocampus, and parts of the hypothalamus and cortex, involved in emotion, motivation, and memory.

**Example:** The limbic system links your memory of a place with the feelings you had there.

**See also:** [Amygdala](#amygdala)

*Taught in [Chapter 11](chapters/11-emotion-and-stress/index.md).*

#### Local Anesthetic

A drug that temporarily blocks feeling in one part of the body by blocking voltage-gated sodium channels in nerve axons.

**Example:** Lidocaine injected before stitches numbs the skin while the patient stays fully awake.

**See also:** [Voltage-Gated Channel](#voltage-gated-channel)

*Taught in [Chapter 2](chapters/02-action-potential/index.md).*

#### Localization of Function

The finding that specific brain regions are specialized for specific jobs.

**Example:** Damage to Broca's area affects producing speech but usually spares understanding it, which shows localization of function.

**See also:** [Lesion Localization](#lesion-localization)

*Taught in [Chapter 4](chapters/04-neuroanatomy/index.md).*

#### Long-Term Memory

Memory that lasts from hours to a lifetime and has a very large capacity.

**Example:** Your memory of your first day of high school is stored in long-term memory.

**See also:** [Declarative Memory](#declarative-memory), [Procedural Memory](#procedural-memory)

*Taught in [Chapter 9](chapters/09-learning-memory-plasticity/index.md).*

#### Long-Term Potentiation

A lasting increase in the strength of a synapse after it has been activated strongly or repeatedly, thought to be a key cellular basis of learning and memory.

**Example:** Long-term potentiation was first described in the hippocampus of rabbits in the early 1970s.

**See also:** [Hebbian Learning](#hebbian-learning), [Neuroplasticity](#neuroplasticity)

*Taught in [Chapter 9](chapters/09-learning-memory-plasticity/index.md).*

#### Lumbar Puncture

A procedure in which a thin needle is placed between bones of the lower back to collect cerebrospinal fluid for testing; it is also called a spinal tap.

**Example:** A lumbar puncture can detect signs of meningitis, such as white blood cells and bacteria in the fluid.

**See also:** [Ventricles and CSF](#ventricles-and-csf), [Meninges](#meninges)

*Taught in [Chapter 16](chapters/16-how-doctors-examine-the-brain/index.md).*

## M

#### Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

#### Mechanoreceptor

#### Melatonin

A hormone released by the pineal gland, mainly in darkness, that signals nighttime to the body and helps time sleep.

#### Membrane Potential

#### Memory

The ability to store information gained from experience and retrieve it later.

*Taught in [Chapter 9](chapters/09-learning-memory-plasticity/index.md).*

#### Memory Consolidation

The process by which new, fragile memories become stable and long-lasting, continuing for hours to years after learning.

*Taught in [Chapter 9](chapters/09-learning-memory-plasticity/index.md).*

#### Meninges

Three protective membranes, the dura mater, arachnoid mater, and pia mater, that wrap the brain and spinal cord.

**Example:** Meningitis is an infection that inflames the meninges, often causing headache, fever, and a stiff neck.

*Taught in [Chapter 4](chapters/04-neuroanatomy/index.md).*

#### Mental Illness

A health condition involving major changes in thinking, emotion, or behavior that cause distress or make it hard to function at school, work, or in relationships.

**Example:** Depression, anxiety disorders, and schizophrenia are mental illnesses, and like other medical conditions, they can be treated.

**See also:** [Stigma and Treatment Access](#stigma-and-treatment-access)

*Taught in [Chapter 15](chapters/15-psychiatric-disorders-and-addiction/index.md).*

#### Mental Status Exam

The part of a neurological or psychiatric assessment that evaluates alertness, orientation, attention, memory, language, mood, and thought.

**Example:** Asking a patient for today's date and to remember three words for later are parts of a mental status exam.

**See also:** [Neurological Exam](#neurological-exam)

*Taught in [Chapter 16](chapters/16-how-doctors-examine-the-brain/index.md).*

#### Metabotropic Receptor

A receptor that does not form a channel but instead starts a chain of chemical steps inside the cell, producing slower and longer-lasting effects.

**Example:** Most dopamine and serotonin receptors are metabotropic receptors.

**See also:** [Ionotropic Receptor](#ionotropic-receptor)

*Taught in [Chapter 3](chapters/03-synapses-and-neurotransmitters/index.md).*

#### Microglia

Small immune cells of the brain and spinal cord that patrol for injury or infection, swallow debris, and help trim unneeded synapses.

**Example:** In Alzheimer's disease, microglia gather around amyloid plaques and try to clear them away.

**See also:** [Glial Cell](#glial-cell)

*Taught in [Chapter 1](chapters/01-neurons-and-glia/index.md).*

#### Motor Learning

The process of making a movement faster, more accurate, and smoother through practice until it becomes more automatic.

**Example:** Learning to dribble a soccer ball without looking down is motor learning.

**See also:** [Procedural Memory](#procedural-memory)

*Taught in [Chapter 7](chapters/07-movement/index.md).*

#### Motor Neuron

A neuron that carries commands from the brain or spinal cord out to muscles or glands.

**Example:** When Maya decides to kick a soccer ball, motor neurons in her spinal cord fire and make her leg muscles contract.

**See also:** [Sensory Neuron](#sensory-neuron), [Upper vs Lower Motor Neuron](#upper-vs-lower-motor-neuron)

*Taught in [Chapter 1](chapters/01-neurons-and-glia/index.md).*

#### Motor Unit

One motor neuron together with all of the muscle fibers it controls.

**Example:** Muscles that move the eyes have small motor units for precise control, while large leg muscles have much bigger ones.

**See also:** [Motor Neuron](#motor-neuron)

*Taught in [Chapter 7](chapters/07-movement/index.md).*

#### Multiple Sclerosis

An autoimmune disease in which the body's immune system attacks myelin in the brain, spinal cord, and optic nerves, slowing or blocking nerve signals.

**Example:** A young adult with multiple sclerosis might have an episode of blurred vision in one eye that improves over several weeks.

**See also:** [Myelin Sheath](#myelin-sheath), [Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)](#magnetic-resonance-imaging-mri)

*Taught in [Chapter 1](chapters/01-neurons-and-glia/index.md).*

#### Myasthenia Gravis

An autoimmune disease in which antibodies attack acetylcholine receptors or related proteins at the neuromuscular junction, causing muscle weakness that worsens with use.

**Example:** A person with myasthenia gravis may have drooping eyelids and double vision that get worse by the end of the day.

**See also:** [Neuromuscular Junction](#neuromuscular-junction)

*Taught in [Chapter 3](chapters/03-synapses-and-neurotransmitters/index.md).*

#### Myelin Sheath

A fatty, insulating wrapping around many axons, made by glial cells, that makes electrical signals travel much faster.

**Example:** In multiple sclerosis, the immune system damages myelin in the brain and spinal cord, so signals slow down or get blocked.

**See also:** [Saltatory Conduction](#saltatory-conduction), [Oligodendrocyte](#oligodendrocyte), [Schwann Cell](#schwann-cell)

*Taught in [Chapter 1](chapters/01-neurons-and-glia/index.md).*

#### Myelination Over Time

The gradual addition of myelin to axons from before birth into early adulthood, which speeds communication between brain regions.

**Example:** Connections of the prefrontal cortex are among the last to finish myelinating, a process that continues into a person's twenties.

**See also:** [Myelin Sheath](#myelin-sheath), [Prefrontal Maturation](#prefrontal-maturation)

*Taught in [Chapter 8](chapters/08-developing-teenage-brain/index.md).*

*Taught in [Chapter 8](chapters/08-developing-teenage-brain/index.md).*

#### Neglect Syndrome

A condition, usually after damage to the right parietal lobe, in which a person fails to notice or respond to things on one side of space, typically the left.

**Example:** A patient with neglect may eat only the food on the right half of the plate.

**See also:** [Parietal Lobe](#parietal-lobe)

*Taught in [Chapter 12](chapters/12-language-attention-decisions/index.md).*

#### Nervous System

The body's network of cells that collects information, processes it, and sends out commands, made up of the brain, spinal cord, and nerves.

**Example:** When you touch a hot pan, your nervous system detects the heat and pulls your hand away in a fraction of a second.

**See also:** [Central Nervous System](#central-nervous-system), [Peripheral Nervous System](#peripheral-nervous-system)

*Taught in [Chapter 1](chapters/01-neurons-and-glia/index.md).*

#### Neural Tube

The hollow tube of tissue in an early embryo that develops into the brain and spinal cord.

**Example:** Getting enough folic acid before and during early pregnancy lowers the risk of neural tube defects such as spina bifida.

*Taught in [Chapter 8](chapters/08-developing-teenage-brain/index.md).*

#### Neurodegeneration

The progressive loss of structure or function of neurons, often ending in their death, which worsens over time.

**Example:** Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease, and ALS are all neurodegenerative diseases.

**See also:** [Protein Misfolding](#protein-misfolding)

*Taught in [Chapter 13](chapters/13-aging-and-neurodegeneration/index.md).*

#### Neurogenesis

The creation of new neurons from dividing stem cells.

**Example:** Most neurogenesis in humans happens before birth, which is why the developing brain is so sensitive to harm during pregnancy.

**See also:** [Neuronal Migration](#neuronal-migration)

*Taught in [Chapter 8](chapters/08-developing-teenage-brain/index.md).*

#### Neurological Exam

A step-by-step bedside check of the nervous system that tests mental status, cranial nerves, strength, reflexes, sensation, coordination, and walking.

**Example:** Asking a patient to walk heel to toe in a straight line tests balance and the cerebellum during a neurological exam.

**See also:** [Mental Status Exam](#mental-status-exam), [Cranial Nerve Exam](#cranial-nerve-exam), [Reflex Testing](#reflex-testing)

*Taught in [Chapter 16](chapters/16-how-doctors-examine-the-brain/index.md).*

#### Neuromuscular Junction

The synapse between a motor neuron and a skeletal muscle fiber, where acetylcholine triggers the muscle fiber to contract.

**Example:** Botulinum toxin blocks the neuromuscular junction, which is how Botox injections relax facial muscles.

**See also:** [Acetylcholine](#acetylcholine), [Myasthenia Gravis](#myasthenia-gravis)

*Taught in [Chapter 3](chapters/03-synapses-and-neurotransmitters/index.md).*

#### Neuron

A cell specialized to receive, process, and send information using electrical and chemical signals.

**Example:** The neurons that let you wiggle your toes have axons that run all the way from the lower spinal cord to the foot.

**See also:** [Glial Cell](#glial-cell)

*Taught in [Chapter 1](chapters/01-neurons-and-glia/index.md).*

#### Neuronal Migration

The movement of newly formed neurons from where they are born to their final positions in the developing brain.

**Example:** When neuronal migration goes wrong, the cortex can form with abnormal folds, which can lead to seizures.

**See also:** [Neurogenesis](#neurogenesis)

*Taught in [Chapter 8](chapters/08-developing-teenage-brain/index.md).*

#### Neuroplasticity

The nervous system's ability to change its structure and function in response to experience, learning, or injury.

**Example:** After a stroke, rehabilitation helps healthy brain areas take over some of the lost functions.

**See also:** [Long-Term Potentiation](#long-term-potentiation)

*Taught in [Chapter 9](chapters/09-learning-memory-plasticity/index.md).*

#### Neuroscience Careers

Jobs that involve studying, treating, or applying knowledge of the nervous system, such as research neuroscientist, neurologist, neurosurgeon, psychiatrist, neuropsychologist, and neuroscience nurse.

**Example:** A neurologist diagnoses and treats brain disorders mainly with medicines, while a neurosurgeon treats them with operations.

**See also:** [Pre-Med Pathway](#pre-med-pathway)

*Taught in [Appendix: Neuroscience Careers](appendices/careers.md).*

#### Neurotoxin

A substance that damages nerve cells or disrupts the way they signal.

**Example:** Tetrodotoxin, found in pufferfish, blocks sodium channels and can cause paralysis.

**See also:** [Local Anesthetic](#local-anesthetic)

*Taught in [Chapter 2](chapters/02-action-potential/index.md).*

#### Neurotransmitter

A chemical messenger that a neuron releases at a synapse to excite, inhibit, or otherwise change the activity of a target cell.

**Example:** Glutamate, GABA, dopamine, and serotonin are all neurotransmitters.

**See also:** [Receptor](#receptor)

*Taught in [Chapter 3](chapters/03-synapses-and-neurotransmitters/index.md).*

#### Nicotine and Vaping

Nicotine is an addictive chemical found in tobacco and most e-cigarettes; vaping is inhaling the aerosol an e-cigarette produces, which often delivers nicotine.

**Example:** Because the teen brain is still developing, nicotine from vaping can harm attention and learning and raise the risk of addiction.

**See also:** [Addiction](#addiction), [Adolescent Brain](#adolescent-brain)

*Taught in [Chapter 15](chapters/15-psychiatric-disorders-and-addiction/index.md).*

#### Nociception

The detection of harmful or possibly harmful stimuli, such as extreme heat, sharp pressure, or chemicals released by damaged tissue, by specialized sensory neurons.

**Example:** Nociception can trigger a withdrawal reflex before you consciously feel any pain.

**See also:** [Pain Pathway](#pain-pathway)

*Taught in [Chapter 6](chapters/06-hearing-touch-taste-smell-pain/index.md).*

#### Node of Ranvier

A short, uncovered gap between sections of myelin along an axon, where ion channels are packed together and the electrical signal is renewed.

**Example:** In a myelinated axon, the signal "jumps" from one node of Ranvier to the next instead of creeping along every bit of membrane.

**See also:** [Saltatory Conduction](#saltatory-conduction)

*Taught in [Chapter 1](chapters/01-neurons-and-glia/index.md).*

#### Norepinephrine

A neurotransmitter and hormone that increases alertness, attention, heart rate, and blood pressure, especially during stress.

**Example:** Norepinephrine released by nerves to the heart makes it pound before a big test.

**See also:** [Fight or Flight](#fight-or-flight)

*Taught in [Chapter 3](chapters/03-synapses-and-neurotransmitters/index.md).*

#### Normal Brain Aging

The gradual brain changes that come with age in healthy people, such as slower processing speed and some loss of brain volume, while knowledge and vocabulary are often kept.

**Example:** An older adult may take longer to recall a name but still knows the meanings of more words than most teenagers.

**See also:** [Dementia](#dementia), [Cognitive Reserve](#cognitive-reserve)

*Taught in [Chapter 13](chapters/13-aging-and-neurodegeneration/index.md).*

#### NREM Sleep (Non-Rapid Eye Movement Sleep)

The part of sleep without rapid eye movements, ranging from light sleep to deep slow-wave sleep, during which brain activity slows down.

**Example:** Sleepwalking usually happens during deep NREM sleep.

**See also:** [REM Sleep (Rapid Eye Movement Sleep)](#rem-sleep-rapid-eye-movement-sleep)

*Taught in [Chapter 10](chapters/10-sleep-and-brain-states/index.md).*

## O

#### Occipital Lobe

The back region of each cerebral hemisphere, devoted mainly to processing vision.

**Example:** A hard hit to the back of the head can make a person "see stars" because the occipital lobe is jolted.

**See also:** [Primary Visual Cortex](#primary-visual-cortex)

*Taught in [Chapter 4](chapters/04-neuroanatomy/index.md).*

#### Olfaction

The sense of smell, in which odor molecules bind receptors in the nose and signals travel to the olfactory bulb and then the brain.

**Example:** A reduced sense of smell can appear years before movement symptoms in some people who later develop Parkinson's disease.

**See also:** [Gustation](#gustation)

*Taught in [Chapter 6](chapters/06-hearing-touch-taste-smell-pain/index.md).*

#### Oligodendrocyte

A glial cell in the brain and spinal cord that wraps myelin around segments of several nearby axons at once.

**Example:** Multiple sclerosis damages myelin made by oligodendrocytes, which is why it affects the brain and spinal cord but not the nerves in your arms.

**See also:** [Schwann Cell](#schwann-cell)

*Taught in [Chapter 1](chapters/01-neurons-and-glia/index.md).*

#### Opioids

A class of drugs, including prescription pain relievers, heroin, and fentanyl, that act on opioid receptors to relieve pain and can slow breathing to dangerous levels.

**Example:** Illegally made fentanyl is involved in many overdose deaths in the United States.

**See also:** [Naloxone](#naloxone), [Agonist and Antagonist](#agonist-and-antagonist)

*Taught in [Chapter 15](chapters/15-psychiatric-disorders-and-addiction/index.md).*

#### Optic Chiasm

The X-shaped crossing point beneath the brain where nerve fibers from the inner (nose-side) half of each retina cross to the opposite side.

**Example:** A tumor pressing on the optic chiasm can cause loss of the outer half of vision in both eyes.

**See also:** [Pituitary Tumor](#pituitary-tumor), [Visual Field Defect](#visual-field-defect)

*Taught in [Chapter 5](chapters/05-vision/index.md).*

#### Optic Nerve

The cranial nerve that carries visual signals from the retina of each eye toward the brain.

**Example:** Glaucoma slowly damages the optic nerve, usually taking away side vision first.

**See also:** [Optic Chiasm](#optic-chiasm)

*Taught in [Chapter 5](chapters/05-vision/index.md).*

#### Orexin (Hypocretin)

A chemical messenger made by neurons in the hypothalamus that promotes wakefulness and keeps the switch between sleep and waking stable.

**Example:** Most people with narcolepsy type 1 have lost the neurons that make orexin.

**See also:** [Narcolepsy](#narcolepsy)

*Taught in [Chapter 10](chapters/10-sleep-and-brain-states/index.md).*

## P

#### Pain Pathway

The route that signals from damage-sensing neurons take into the spinal cord, up through the brainstem and thalamus, to brain areas that create the experience of pain.

**Example:** Opioid medicines reduce pain by acting at several points along the pain pathway.

**See also:** [Nociception](#nociception), [Gate Control Theory](#gate-control-theory)

*Taught in [Chapter 6](chapters/06-hearing-touch-taste-smell-pain/index.md).*

#### Parietal Lobe

The upper back region of each cerebral hemisphere, which processes touch and body position and helps track where things are in space.

**Example:** Damage to the right parietal lobe can make a person ignore the left side of space.

**See also:** [Neglect Syndrome](#neglect-syndrome)

*Taught in [Chapter 4](chapters/04-neuroanatomy/index.md).*

#### Parkinson's Disease

A progressive brain disorder caused mainly by the loss of dopamine-making neurons in the substantia nigra, leading to tremor at rest, stiffness, and slow movement.

**Example:** An early sign can be a slight tremor in one hand and handwriting that grows smaller across the page.

**See also:** [Levodopa](#levodopa), [Deep Brain Stimulation](#deep-brain-stimulation)

*Taught in [Chapter 7](chapters/07-movement/index.md).*

#### Patient H.M.

Henry Molaison, a man who had parts of both medial temporal lobes, including much of the hippocampus, removed in 1953 to treat severe epilepsy and who afterward could not form new declarative memories.

**Example:** H.M. got better at a mirror-drawing task over several days without remembering that he had practiced, showing that procedural memory relies on different brain systems.

**See also:** [Hippocampus](#hippocampus), [Amnesia](#amnesia)

*Taught in [Chapter 9](chapters/09-learning-memory-plasticity/index.md).*

#### Peripheral Nervous System

All the nerves and nerve cells outside the brain and spinal cord, which link them to the skin, muscles, and internal organs.

**Example:** Numbness and tingling in the feet caused by long-term diabetes is a peripheral nerve problem, not a brain problem.

**See also:** [Central Nervous System](#central-nervous-system)

*Taught in [Chapter 1](chapters/01-neurons-and-glia/index.md).*

#### PET Scan (Positron Emission Tomography)

An imaging test that detects a weakly radioactive tracer injected into the blood to show metabolism, blood flow, or specific molecules in the brain.

**Example:** An amyloid PET scan can show whether amyloid plaques are present before a patient starts anti-amyloid treatment.

**See also:** [Amyloid Plaque](#amyloid-plaque), [Biomarker](#biomarker)

*Taught in [Chapter 16](chapters/16-how-doctors-examine-the-brain/index.md).*

#### Phantom Limb Pain

Pain that a person feels as if it comes from a body part that has been amputated.

**Example:** Mirror therapy, in which a person watches the reflection of their remaining limb move, eases phantom limb pain for some people.

**See also:** [Neuroplasticity](#neuroplasticity)

*Taught in [Chapter 6](chapters/06-hearing-touch-taste-smell-pain/index.md).*

#### Photoreceptor

A light-sensing cell in the retina that absorbs light and converts it into an electrical signal.

**Example:** Rods and cones are the two types of photoreceptors used for vision in the human eye.

**See also:** [Rods and Cones](#rods-and-cones)

*Taught in [Chapter 5](chapters/05-vision/index.md).*

#### Pituitary Tumor

An abnormal growth, usually not cancer, in the pituitary gland at the base of the brain that can disturb hormone levels or press on nearby structures.

**Example:** A large pituitary tumor can press on the optic chiasm and take away side vision in both eyes.

**See also:** [Optic Chiasm](#optic-chiasm)

*Taught in [Chapter 5](chapters/05-vision/index.md).*

#### Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD)

A mental health condition that can develop after a terrifying event, involving unwanted memories or flashbacks, avoidance of reminders, negative changes in mood, and feeling constantly on alert.

**Example:** A person with PTSD after a car crash may avoid driving and startle easily at loud noises; therapy and medication can help.

**See also:** [Fear Conditioning](#fear-conditioning), [Amygdala](#amygdala)

*Taught in [Chapter 11](chapters/11-emotion-and-stress/index.md).*

#### Pre-Med Pathway

The usual route to medical school in the United States: a bachelor's degree that includes required science courses, clinical and research experience, and the MCAT admissions exam.

**Example:** A pre-med student might major in neuroscience, volunteer at a hospital, and take the MCAT before applying to medical school.

**See also:** [Neuroscience Careers](#neuroscience-careers)

*Taught in [Appendix: Neuroscience Careers](appendices/careers.md).*

#### Prefrontal Cortex

The front-most part of the frontal lobe, which supports planning, judgment, impulse control, working memory, and social behavior.

**Example:** The prefrontal cortex helps you resist checking your phone in the middle of an exam.

**See also:** [Executive Function](#executive-function), [Prefrontal Maturation](#prefrontal-maturation)

*Taught in [Chapter 4](chapters/04-neuroanatomy/index.md).*

#### Prefrontal Maturation

The slow development of the prefrontal cortex and its connections, which continues through adolescence into early adulthood and supports growing self-control and planning.

**Example:** Prefrontal maturation helps explain why older teens are generally better than younger teens at resisting impulses.

**See also:** [Prefrontal Cortex](#prefrontal-cortex), [Adolescent Brain](#adolescent-brain)

*Taught in [Chapter 8](chapters/08-developing-teenage-brain/index.md).*

#### Primary Motor Cortex

The strip of cerebral cortex at the back of the frontal lobe that sends commands for voluntary movement.

**Example:** A stroke in the left primary motor cortex can weaken the right arm.

**See also:** [Corticospinal Tract](#corticospinal-tract), [Homunculus](#homunculus)

*Taught in [Chapter 7](chapters/07-movement/index.md).*

#### Primary Visual Cortex

The first area of the cerebral cortex to receive visual information, located in the occipital lobe, where basic features such as edges and their angles are detected.

**Example:** A stroke in the left primary visual cortex causes loss of the right half of vision in both eyes.

**See also:** [Ventral and Dorsal Streams](#ventral-and-dorsal-streams)

*Taught in [Chapter 5](chapters/05-vision/index.md).*

#### Procedural Memory

Memory for skills and habits, shown by performing them rather than by describing them.

**Example:** Typing without looking at the keyboard relies on procedural memory.

**See also:** [Declarative Memory](#declarative-memory), [Motor Learning](#motor-learning)

*Taught in [Chapter 9](chapters/09-learning-memory-plasticity/index.md).*

#### Proprioception

The sense of the position and movement of your own body parts, based on receptors in muscles, tendons, and joints.

**Example:** Proprioception lets you touch your nose with your eyes closed.

**See also:** [Vestibular System](#vestibular-system)

*Taught in [Chapter 6](chapters/06-hearing-touch-taste-smell-pain/index.md).*

#### Protein

A large molecule built from a chain of amino acids that folds into a specific shape, which lets it do a specific job such as forming structures, speeding up chemical reactions, or passing on signals.

**Example:** Ion channels, receptors, and the enzymes that make neurotransmitters are all proteins.

**See also:** [Protein Misfolding](#protein-misfolding)

*Taught in [Chapter 1](chapters/01-neurons-and-glia/index.md).*

#### Protein Misfolding

An error in which a protein folds into the wrong shape, so it loses its normal function and may clump together in ways that harm cells.

**Example:** In Alzheimer's disease, misfolded amyloid-beta and tau proteins build up in the brain.

**See also:** [Amyloid Plaque](#amyloid-plaque), [Tau Tangle](#tau-tangle)

*Taught in [Chapter 13](chapters/13-aging-and-neurodegeneration/index.md).*

#### Psychiatric Medication

A medicine used to treat mental illness by changing chemical signaling in the brain.

**Example:** Antidepressants, antipsychotics, mood stabilizers, and stimulants for ADHD are all psychiatric medications.

**See also:** [Reuptake](#reuptake)

*Taught in [Chapter 15](chapters/15-psychiatric-disorders-and-addiction/index.md).*

## R

#### Receptive Field

The specific part of the sensory world, such as a patch of skin or a region of what you see, in which a stimulus changes a particular sensory neuron's activity.

**Example:** Touch neurons in your fingertips have tiny receptive fields, which is why fingertips can feel fine details like Braille dots.

*Taught in [Chapter 5](chapters/05-vision/index.md).*

#### Receptor

A protein that recognizes and binds a specific signaling molecule, such as a neurotransmitter, hormone, or drug, and causes a change in the cell.

**Example:** Opioid drugs act by binding opioid receptors on neurons.

**See also:** [Ionotropic Receptor](#ionotropic-receptor), [Metabotropic Receptor](#metabotropic-receptor)

*Taught in [Chapter 3](chapters/03-synapses-and-neurotransmitters/index.md).*

#### Reflex Arc

The neural circuit behind a reflex: a sensory receptor, a sensory neuron, a connection in the spinal cord or brainstem, a motor neuron, and a muscle or gland.

**Example:** Pulling your hand from a hot stove uses a reflex arc through the spinal cord before your brain even registers the pain.

**See also:** [Stretch Reflex](#stretch-reflex)

*Taught in [Chapter 7](chapters/07-movement/index.md).*

#### Reflex Testing

Checking automatic responses, such as tapping a tendon with a reflex hammer, to assess sensory and motor nerves and the spinal cord and to spot signs of brain or nerve damage.

**Example:** In an adult, a big toe that points upward when the sole of the foot is stroked (the Babinski sign) suggests upper motor neuron damage.

**See also:** [Stretch Reflex](#stretch-reflex), [Upper vs Lower Motor Neuron](#upper-vs-lower-motor-neuron)

*Taught in [Chapter 16](chapters/16-how-doctors-examine-the-brain/index.md).*

#### Refractory Period

A short time after an action potential during which a neuron cannot fire again at all (absolute) or needs a stronger push to fire (relative).

**Example:** The refractory period keeps an action potential moving forward down the axon instead of bouncing backward.

**See also:** [Action Potential](#action-potential)

*Taught in [Chapter 2](chapters/02-action-potential/index.md).*

#### REM Sleep (Rapid Eye Movement Sleep)

A stage of sleep with quick eye movements, active brain waves, vivid dreams, and temporary paralysis of most skeletal muscles.

**Example:** Muscle paralysis during REM sleep keeps you from acting out your dreams.

**See also:** [NREM Sleep (Non-Rapid Eye Movement Sleep)](#nrem-sleep-non-rapid-eye-movement-sleep)

*Taught in [Chapter 10](chapters/10-sleep-and-brain-states/index.md).*

#### Repolarization

The return of the membrane potential toward its negative resting level after depolarization, caused mainly by potassium ions flowing out of the cell.

**Example:** Right after the peak of an action potential, potassium channels open and repolarization begins.

**See also:** [Depolarization](#depolarization)

*Taught in [Chapter 2](chapters/02-action-potential/index.md).*

#### Research Ethics

The principles that guide responsible research, including informed consent, minimizing harm, protecting privacy, and the humane treatment of animals.

**Example:** Before a teen joins a brain imaging study, researchers explain the risks, and the teen agrees to take part along with a parent's permission.

**See also:** [Scientific Method in Neuroscience](#scientific-method-in-neuroscience)

*Taught in [Chapter 9](chapters/09-learning-memory-plasticity/index.md).*

#### Resting Potential

The steady membrane potential of a neuron that is not sending a signal, typically about −70 millivolts, with the inside negative compared with the outside.

**Example:** A very high potassium level in the blood makes the resting potential less negative, which can disturb how nerves and the heart work.

**See also:** [Sodium-Potassium Pump](#sodium-potassium-pump)

*Taught in [Chapter 2](chapters/02-action-potential/index.md).*

#### Return-to-Learn and Play

The gradual, step-by-step process of resuming school work and then sports after a concussion as symptoms allow.

**Example:** A student might start with shorter school days and less screen time, return to full classes, and only then begin light exercise before full practice.

**See also:** [Concussion](#concussion)

*Taught in [Chapter 14](chapters/14-injury-stroke-epilepsy/index.md).*

#### Reuptake

The process in which a neuron pulls released neurotransmitter back into its axon terminal through transporter proteins, ending the signal.

**Example:** Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), a common type of antidepressant, block reuptake of serotonin.

**See also:** [Serotonin](#serotonin)

*Taught in [Chapter 3](chapters/03-synapses-and-neurotransmitters/index.md).*

#### Reward Pathway

A brain circuit of dopamine neurons running from the midbrain (ventral tegmental area) to the nucleus accumbens and prefrontal cortex that drives motivation toward rewarding things.

**Example:** Eating good food, spending time with friends, and taking addictive drugs all activate the reward pathway.

**See also:** [Dopamine](#dopamine), [Addiction](#addiction)

*Taught in [Chapter 15](chapters/15-psychiatric-disorders-and-addiction/index.md).*

#### Reward Prediction Error

The difference between the reward that was expected and the reward actually received, signaled by changes in the firing of dopamine neurons.

**Example:** Dopamine neurons fire more when a surprise reward arrives and fire less when an expected reward fails to show up.

**See also:** [Dopamine](#dopamine), [Decision-Making](#decision-making)

*Taught in [Chapter 12](chapters/12-language-attention-decisions/index.md).*

#### Reward Sensitivity in Teens

The stronger response of the adolescent brain's reward circuits to excitement, new experiences, and social approval.

**Example:** In experiments, teens take more risks when friends are watching, partly because peers boost reward signals in the brain.

**See also:** [Reward Pathway](#reward-pathway), [Adolescent Brain](#adolescent-brain)

*Taught in [Chapter 8](chapters/08-developing-teenage-brain/index.md).*

#### Rods and Cones

Rods are photoreceptors that work in dim light and see only shades of gray; cones work in bright light and provide color and sharp detail.

**Example:** At night you can spot a faint star better out of the corner of your eye, because rods are concentrated away from the center of the retina.

**See also:** [Color Vision](#color-vision)

*Taught in [Chapter 5](chapters/05-vision/index.md).*

## S

#### Saltatory Conduction

The way an action potential travels along a myelinated axon by jumping from one node of Ranvier to the next.

**Example:** Saltatory conduction lets signals from your toes reach your spinal cord fast enough for quick reflexes.

**See also:** [Myelin Sheath](#myelin-sheath), [Conduction Velocity](#conduction-velocity)

*Taught in [Chapter 2](chapters/02-action-potential/index.md).*

#### Schizophrenia

A serious mental illness that affects how a person thinks, perceives, and behaves, often including hallucinations, delusions, disorganized thinking, and reduced motivation.

**Example:** Schizophrenia usually first appears in late adolescence or early adulthood, and treatment helps many people manage their symptoms.

**See also:** [Psychiatric Medication](#psychiatric-medication)

*Taught in [Chapter 15](chapters/15-psychiatric-disorders-and-addiction/index.md).*

#### Schwann Cell

A glial cell outside the brain and spinal cord that wraps myelin around one segment of a single axon and helps damaged nerves regrow.

**Example:** Because Schwann cells guide repair, a cut nerve in a finger can slowly regain feeling, while a damaged spinal cord usually cannot.

**See also:** [Oligodendrocyte](#oligodendrocyte)

*Taught in [Chapter 1](chapters/01-neurons-and-glia/index.md).*

#### Scientific Method in Neuroscience

The process of asking a question, forming a testable hypothesis, running controlled experiments, collecting data, and revising ideas based on the results, applied to the nervous system.

**Example:** To test whether sleep helps memory, researchers might compare word recall in a group that sleeps after studying with a group that stays awake.

**See also:** [Correlation vs Causation](#correlation-vs-causation), [Animal Models](#animal-models)

*Taught in [Chapter 9](chapters/09-learning-memory-plasticity/index.md).*

#### Seizure

A sudden burst of abnormal, excessive electrical activity in the brain that temporarily changes movement, sensation, awareness, or behavior.

**Example:** A seizure can look like whole-body jerking, or simply like a brief blank stare.

**See also:** [Epilepsy](#epilepsy), [Seizure First Aid](#seizure-first-aid)

*Taught in [Chapter 14](chapters/14-injury-stroke-epilepsy/index.md).*

#### Seizure First Aid

The steps a bystander takes to keep a person safe during a seizure, such as timing it, moving hard objects away, cushioning the head, turning the person on their side, and never putting anything in their mouth.

**Example:** If a seizure lasts longer than 5 minutes, call 911.

**See also:** [Seizure](#seizure)

*Taught in [Chapter 14](chapters/14-injury-stroke-epilepsy/index.md).*

#### Sensory Neuron

A neuron that carries information from the body or the outside world toward the brain and spinal cord.

**Example:** When a mosquito lands on your arm, sensory neurons in your skin send that touch signal to your spinal cord and brain.

**See also:** [Motor Neuron](#motor-neuron), [Interneuron](#interneuron)

*Taught in [Chapter 1](chapters/01-neurons-and-glia/index.md).*

#### Sensory Transduction

The conversion of a physical or chemical stimulus, such as light, sound, or pressure, into electrical signals in a sensory cell.

**Example:** In the eye, photoreceptors transduce light into changes in their membrane potential.

**See also:** [Photoreceptor](#photoreceptor), [Mechanoreceptor](#mechanoreceptor)

*Taught in [Chapter 5](chapters/05-vision/index.md).*

#### Serotonin

A neurotransmitter that helps regulate mood, sleep, appetite, and digestion.

**Example:** Many antidepressants work by increasing the amount of serotonin available at synapses.

**See also:** [Reuptake](#reuptake)

*Taught in [Chapter 3](chapters/03-synapses-and-neurotransmitters/index.md).*

#### Short-Term Memory

A brief, limited store that holds a small amount of information for seconds to under a minute unless it is rehearsed.

**Example:** Holding a verification code in mind just long enough to type it uses short-term memory.

**See also:** [Working Memory](#working-memory)

*Taught in [Chapter 9](chapters/09-learning-memory-plasticity/index.md).*

#### Skeletal Muscle Contraction

The shortening of a skeletal muscle fiber, triggered by a nerve signal that releases calcium inside the fiber and lets protein filaments (actin and myosin) slide past each other.

**Example:** Every sprint Maya makes at soccer practice depends on skeletal muscle contraction in her legs.

**See also:** [Neuromuscular Junction](#neuromuscular-junction), [Motor Unit](#motor-unit)

*Taught in [Chapter 7](chapters/07-movement/index.md).*

#### Sleep and Memory

The relationship in which sleep helps stabilize, strengthen, and reorganize memories formed while awake.

**Example:** Students who sleep after learning new vocabulary usually remember more the next day than students who stay awake.

**See also:** [Memory Consolidation](#memory-consolidation)

*Taught in [Chapter 10](chapters/10-sleep-and-brain-states/index.md).*

#### Sleep Deprivation

A condition of not getting enough sleep, either all at once or over many nights, which impairs attention, mood, memory, and reaction time.

**Example:** Drowsy driving is dangerous because sleep deprivation slows reaction time.

**See also:** [Teen Sleep Needs](#teen-sleep-needs)

*Taught in [Chapter 10](chapters/10-sleep-and-brain-states/index.md).*

#### Sleep Stages

The repeating phases of sleep, made up of three stages of non-rapid eye movement sleep and one stage of rapid eye movement sleep, which cycle several times each night.

**Example:** One full cycle through the sleep stages takes roughly 90 minutes.

**See also:** [NREM Sleep (Non-Rapid Eye Movement Sleep)](#nrem-sleep-non-rapid-eye-movement-sleep), [REM Sleep (Rapid Eye Movement Sleep)](#rem-sleep-rapid-eye-movement-sleep)

*Taught in [Chapter 10](chapters/10-sleep-and-brain-states/index.md).*

#### Sodium-Potassium Pump

A membrane protein that uses the cell's chemical energy (ATP) to move three sodium ions out of the cell and two potassium ions in, keeping the ion differences neurons need.

**Example:** A large share of the brain's energy use goes to running sodium-potassium pumps.

**See also:** [Concentration Gradient](#concentration-gradient), [Resting Potential](#resting-potential)

*Taught in [Chapter 2](chapters/02-action-potential/index.md).*

#### Somatosensory Cortex

The strip of cerebral cortex at the front of the parietal lobe, just behind the primary motor cortex, that receives and processes touch and body-position information.

**Example:** Gently stimulating the somatosensory cortex during brain surgery can make an awake patient feel a tingle in one specific body part.

**See also:** [Homunculus](#homunculus)

*Taught in [Chapter 6](chapters/06-hearing-touch-taste-smell-pain/index.md).*

#### Somatosensory System

The sensory system that detects touch, pressure, vibration, temperature, pain, and body position from the skin, muscles, and joints.

**Example:** Recognizing a coin in your pocket without looking uses your somatosensory system.

**See also:** [Mechanoreceptor](#mechanoreceptor), [Somatosensory Cortex](#somatosensory-cortex)

*Taught in [Chapter 6](chapters/06-hearing-touch-taste-smell-pain/index.md).*

#### Spinal Cord

A column of nerve tissue running inside the backbone that carries signals between the brain and body and controls some reflexes on its own.

**Example:** An injury to the spinal cord in the neck can affect movement and feeling in both the arms and the legs.

**See also:** [Spinal Cord Injury](#spinal-cord-injury), [Reflex Arc](#reflex-arc)

*Taught in [Chapter 4](chapters/04-neuroanatomy/index.md).*

#### Spinal Cord Injury

Damage to the spinal cord that disrupts signals between the brain and the body below the injury, causing loss of movement, sensation, or automatic functions.

**Example:** An injury in the neck region can affect all four limbs, while one in the lower back may affect only the legs.

**See also:** [Spinal Cord](#spinal-cord), [Dermatome](#dermatome)

*Taught in [Chapter 14](chapters/14-injury-stroke-epilepsy/index.md).*

#### Split-Brain Studies

Research on people whose corpus callosum was surgically cut to treat severe epilepsy, which revealed how each hemisphere works on its own.

**Example:** A split-brain patient could not name an object shown only in the left visual field, because that information reached only the right hemisphere.

**See also:** [Corpus Callosum](#corpus-callosum), [Hemispheric Lateralization](#hemispheric-lateralization)

*Taught in [Chapter 12](chapters/12-language-attention-decisions/index.md).*

#### Stigma and Treatment Access

Stigma is negative attitudes or stereotypes about people with a condition; together with cost and shortages of providers, it can keep people from getting care.

**Example:** Using person-first language, such as "a person with a substance use disorder," helps reduce stigma.

**See also:** [Mental Illness](#mental-illness)

*Taught in [Chapter 15](chapters/15-psychiatric-disorders-and-addiction/index.md).*

#### Stress Response

The body's set of reactions to a challenge or threat, including fast nerve signals that raise heart rate and slower hormone signals that release stored energy.

**Example:** Before a major exam, your stress response sharpens attention and makes your heart beat faster.

**See also:** [HPA Axis (Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal Axis)](#hpa-axis-hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal-axis), [Fight or Flight](#fight-or-flight)

*Taught in [Chapter 11](chapters/11-emotion-and-stress/index.md).*

#### Stretch Reflex

An automatic muscle contraction that happens when a muscle is suddenly stretched, detected by stretch sensors called muscle spindles.

**Example:** The knee-jerk response to a tap below the kneecap is a stretch reflex.

**See also:** [Reflex Testing](#reflex-testing)

*Taught in [Chapter 7](chapters/07-movement/index.md).*

#### Stroke

A sudden loss of brain function caused by interrupted blood flow to part of the brain or by bleeding in or around the brain.

**Example:** Sudden weakness on one side of the face and body is a common sign of stroke.

**See also:** [Ischemic Stroke](#ischemic-stroke), [Hemorrhagic Stroke](#hemorrhagic-stroke), [BE-FAST Warning Signs](#be-fast-warning-signs)

*Taught in [Chapter 14](chapters/14-injury-stroke-epilepsy/index.md).*

#### Substantia Nigra

A dark-colored region in the midbrain whose neurons supply dopamine to the basal ganglia.

**Example:** In Parkinson's disease, the substantia nigra loses its dark color as its dopamine neurons die.

**See also:** [Parkinson's Disease](#parkinsons-disease)

*Taught in [Chapter 7](chapters/07-movement/index.md).*

#### Suprachiasmatic Nucleus

A tiny cluster of neurons in the hypothalamus, just above the optic chiasm, that acts as the body's master clock.

**Example:** Morning light signals from the eyes reach the suprachiasmatic nucleus and reset the body clock each day.

**See also:** [Circadian Rhythm](#circadian-rhythm)

*Taught in [Chapter 10](chapters/10-sleep-and-brain-states/index.md).*

#### Sympathetic vs Parasympathetic

The two main branches of the autonomic nervous system: the sympathetic branch prepares the body for action during danger or exercise, and the parasympathetic branch supports rest and digestion.

**Example:** Before a penalty kick, sympathetic activity speeds Maya's heart; after the game, parasympathetic activity slows it back down.

**See also:** [Fight or Flight](#fight-or-flight)

*Taught in [Chapter 4](chapters/04-neuroanatomy/index.md).*

#### Synapse

The junction where one neuron passes a signal to another neuron, a muscle cell, or a gland cell.

**Example:** Learning a new skill strengthens some synapses and weakens others.

**See also:** [Chemical Synapse](#chemical-synapse), [Electrical Synapse](#electrical-synapse)

*Taught in [Chapter 3](chapters/03-synapses-and-neurotransmitters/index.md).*

#### Synaptic Integration

The process by which a neuron combines all the excitatory and inhibitory inputs it receives at a given moment, which determines whether it fires.

**Example:** If EPSPs outweigh IPSPs enough to reach threshold at the start of the axon, the neuron fires.

*Taught in [Chapter 3](chapters/03-synapses-and-neurotransmitters/index.md).*

#### Synaptic Pruning

The removal of weak or unused synapses during development, which makes brain circuits more efficient.

**Example:** During the teen years, pruning in the prefrontal cortex helps fine-tune circuits for planning and judgment.

**See also:** [Synaptogenesis](#synaptogenesis), [Adolescent Brain](#adolescent-brain)

*Taught in [Chapter 8](chapters/08-developing-teenage-brain/index.md).*

#### Synaptic Vesicle

A tiny membrane bubble inside an axon terminal that stores neurotransmitter molecules and releases them by fusing with the cell membrane.

**Example:** Calcium flowing into the axon terminal triggers synaptic vesicles to empty their contents into the synapse.

**See also:** [Axon Terminal](#axon-terminal)

*Taught in [Chapter 3](chapters/03-synapses-and-neurotransmitters/index.md).*

#### Synaptogenesis

The formation of new synapses between neurons.

**Example:** Synaptogenesis is especially fast in infancy, when the brain forms enormous numbers of new connections.

**See also:** [Synaptic Pruning](#synaptic-pruning)

*Taught in [Chapter 8](chapters/08-developing-teenage-brain/index.md).*

## T

#### Tau Tangle

A twisted bundle of abnormal tau protein that builds up inside neurons and is a hallmark of Alzheimer's disease and some other dementias.

**Example:** The spread of tau tangles across the brain tracks closely with worsening memory and thinking.

**See also:** [Amyloid Plaque](#amyloid-plaque), [Chronic Traumatic Encephalopathy (CTE)](#chronic-traumatic-encephalopathy-cte)

*Taught in [Chapter 13](chapters/13-aging-and-neurodegeneration/index.md).*

#### Teen Sleep Needs

The amount and timing of sleep that adolescents need, shaped by a body clock that naturally shifts later during the teen years.

**Example:** Sleep experts at the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommend 8 to 10 hours of sleep a day for teens aged 13 to 18, so a teen whose body clock makes it hard to fall asleep before 11 p.m. can easily come up short when school starts early.

**See also:** [Circadian Rhythm](#circadian-rhythm), [Sleep Deprivation](#sleep-deprivation)

*Taught in [Chapter 10](chapters/10-sleep-and-brain-states/index.md).*

#### Temporal Lobe

The region on the lower side of each cerebral hemisphere, near the ears, involved in hearing, understanding language, recognizing objects and faces, and forming memories.

**Example:** Seizures that begin in the temporal lobe often start with an odd feeling such as déjà vu.

**See also:** [Hippocampus](#hippocampus)

*Taught in [Chapter 4](chapters/04-neuroanatomy/index.md).*

#### Teratogen

A substance, infection, or other factor that can cause birth defects or harm development when an embryo or fetus is exposed to it.

**Example:** Alcohol, certain medicines, and the Zika virus are teratogens that can affect brain development.

**See also:** [Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorders (FASD)](#fetal-alcohol-spectrum-disorders-fasd)

*Taught in [Chapter 8](chapters/08-developing-teenage-brain/index.md).*

#### Thalamus

A pair of egg-shaped structures deep in the brain that relay and filter sensory and motor signals on their way to the cerebral cortex.

**Example:** Almost all sensory information except smell passes through the thalamus before reaching the cortex.

**See also:** [Hypothalamus](#hypothalamus)

*Taught in [Chapter 4](chapters/04-neuroanatomy/index.md).*

#### Threshold

The membrane potential, often around −55 millivolts, at which enough voltage-gated sodium channels open to set off an action potential.

**Example:** A very light brush may depolarize a touch neuron a little without reaching threshold, so no signal is sent.

**See also:** [All-or-None Principle](#all-or-none-principle)

*Taught in [Chapter 2](chapters/02-action-potential/index.md).*

#### Tolerance and Withdrawal

Tolerance is needing more of a drug to get the same effect; withdrawal is the set of unpleasant symptoms that appear when a person stops or cuts back on a drug the body has adapted to.

**Example:** A daily coffee drinker may need more coffee to feel alert (tolerance) and get a headache on a day without it (withdrawal).

**See also:** [Addiction](#addiction)

*Taught in [Chapter 15](chapters/15-psychiatric-disorders-and-addiction/index.md).*

#### Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI)

Damage to the brain caused by an outside force, such as a blow, a jolt, or an object entering the skull, ranging from mild to severe.

**Example:** Falls and motor vehicle crashes are common causes of traumatic brain injury.

**See also:** [Concussion](#concussion)

*Taught in [Chapter 14](chapters/14-injury-stroke-epilepsy/index.md).*

## U

#### Upper vs Lower Motor Neuron

Upper motor neurons run from the brain to the brainstem or spinal cord; lower motor neurons run from the brainstem or spinal cord to the muscles, and damage to each gives a different pattern of signs.

**Example:** Upper motor neuron damage usually causes stiff muscles and brisk reflexes, while lower motor neuron damage causes floppy, shrinking muscles and weak reflexes.

**See also:** [Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS)](#amyotrophic-lateral-sclerosis-als)

*Taught in [Chapter 7](chapters/07-movement/index.md).*

## V

#### Ventral and Dorsal Streams

Two main routes of visual processing beyond the primary visual cortex: the ventral ("what") stream through the temporal lobe identifies objects, and the dorsal ("where/how") stream through the parietal lobe tracks location and guides action.

**Example:** A person with ventral stream damage may be unable to name a cup yet still reach for it accurately.

**See also:** [Primary Visual Cortex](#primary-visual-cortex)

*Taught in [Chapter 5](chapters/05-vision/index.md).*

#### Ventricles and CSF

The ventricles are connected, fluid-filled spaces inside the brain; cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is the clear fluid made there that cushions and bathes the brain and spinal cord.

**Example:** If CSF cannot drain properly, the ventricles swell, a condition called hydrocephalus.

**See also:** [Lumbar Puncture](#lumbar-puncture)

*Taught in [Chapter 4](chapters/04-neuroanatomy/index.md).*

#### Vestibular System

The sensory system in the inner ear that detects head movement, tilt, and gravity to support balance and keep vision steady.

**Example:** Spinning around and then stopping makes you dizzy because fluid in your vestibular system keeps moving for a moment.

**See also:** [Proprioception](#proprioception)

*Taught in [Chapter 6](chapters/06-hearing-touch-taste-smell-pain/index.md).*

#### Visual Field

The whole area a person can see at one time while keeping the eyes fixed on a single point.

**Example:** A doctor tests the visual field by asking a patient to look at the doctor's nose and say when a wiggling finger appears from the side.

**See also:** [Visual Field Defect](#visual-field-defect)

*Taught in [Chapter 5](chapters/05-vision/index.md).*

#### Visual Field Defect

A loss of vision in part of the visual field caused by damage somewhere along the visual pathway, from the eye to the brain.

**Example:** Loss of the outer halves of vision in both eyes (bitemporal hemianopia) points to damage at the optic chiasm.

**See also:** [Visual Field](#visual-field), [Lesion Localization](#lesion-localization)

*Taught in [Chapter 5](chapters/05-vision/index.md).*

#### Visual Pathway

The route visual signals take from the retina through the optic nerve, optic chiasm, optic tract, and thalamus to the primary visual cortex.

**Example:** Doctors map which part of vision is lost to work out where along the visual pathway the damage lies.

**See also:** [Visual Field Defect](#visual-field-defect)

*Taught in [Chapter 5](chapters/05-vision/index.md).*

#### Voltage-Gated Channel

An ion channel that opens or closes in response to changes in the membrane potential.

**Example:** Voltage-gated sodium channels snap open when a neuron reaches threshold, causing the rising phase of an action potential.

**See also:** [Threshold](#threshold)

*Taught in [Chapter 2](chapters/02-action-potential/index.md).*

## W

#### Wernicke's Area

A region in the upper back part of the temporal lobe, usually on the left, that is important for understanding language.

**Example:** Damage to Wernicke's area can cause fluent speech that makes little sense, along with trouble understanding others.

**See also:** [Broca's Area](#brocas-area), [Aphasia](#aphasia)

*Taught in [Chapter 12](chapters/12-language-attention-decisions/index.md).*

#### White Matter

Brain and spinal cord tissue made mostly of myelinated axons that connect regions of gray matter; the fatty myelin gives it a pale color.

**Example:** The corpus callosum is the largest bundle of white matter in the brain.

**See also:** [Gray Matter](#gray-matter)

*Taught in [Chapter 4](chapters/04-neuroanatomy/index.md).*

#### Working Memory

The ability to hold information in mind while actively using or changing it.

**Example:** Figuring out a 20 percent tip in your head uses working memory.

**See also:** [Short-Term Memory](#short-term-memory), [Executive Function](#executive-function)

*Taught in [Chapter 9](chapters/09-learning-memory-plasticity/index.md).*
