# References: Sleep and Brain States

1. [Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep](https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep) - National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke (NIH) - A clear overview of NREM and REM sleep, the brain structures and chemicals that control sleep (including the SCN, melatonin, adenosine, and orexin), and how sleep needs change with age. A great first stop for reviewing this whole chapter.

2. [Narcolepsy](https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/disorders/narcolepsy) - National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke (NIH) - Explains the symptoms, types, causes, diagnostic tests, and current treatments for narcolepsy, including the 2026 approval of an orexin-based medicine. Useful background for the Bedside case.

3. [About Sleep](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html) - Centers for Disease Control and Prevention - Lists recommended hours of sleep for every age group, including 8 to 10 hours for teens, with links to the expert statements behind each number. Handy for checking your own sleep target.

4. [Recommended Amount of Sleep for Pediatric Populations: A Consensus Statement of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4877308/) - Paruthi and colleagues, *Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine* (2016) - The short, official expert statement that sets the recommendation of 8 to 10 hours for ages 13 to 18 and summarizes the health risks of regularly getting less. It's only two pages, so it's a good first research paper to read.

5. [School Start Times for Middle School and High School Students: United States, 2011-12 School Year](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/mm6430a1.htm) - CDC, *Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report* (2015) - Reports how early U.S. middle and high schools start and explains why the teen circadian clock shifts later during puberty. Useful evidence for any school start time debate.

6. [15.3 Regulation of Sleep](https://openstax.org/books/introduction-behavioral-neuroscience/pages/15-3-regulation-of-sleep) - OpenStax, *Introduction to Behavioral Neuroscience* (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0) - A free college-level textbook section on the two-process model, sleep stages, sleep cycles, and the flip-flop switch. Read it when you want more depth than this chapter gives.

7. [15.2 Where Are Rhythms in the Brain?](https://openstax.org/books/introduction-behavioral-neuroscience/pages/15-2-where-are-rhythms-in-the-brain) - OpenStax, *Introduction to Behavioral Neuroscience* (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0) - Covers the suprachiasmatic nucleus, light-sensing retinal cells and melanopsin, clock genes, and melatonin. Good for students who want to understand how a group of cells can keep time.

8. [What Are Sleep Deprivation and Deficiency?](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/sleep-deprivation) - National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NIH) - Describes how too little sleep affects learning, mood, safety, and long-term health. A clear, trustworthy source for health class projects on sleep.

9. [About Sleep's Role in Memory](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3768102/) - Rasch and Born, *Physiological Reviews* (2013) - A detailed review of how slow-wave sleep, sleep spindles, and REM sleep may help consolidate memories. It's advanced, but the introduction and figures are readable for motivated high school students.

10. [Reduced Number of Hypocretin Neurons in Human Narcolepsy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8760623/) - Thannickal and colleagues, *Neuron* (2000) - The landmark study showing that people with narcolepsy have lost 85 to 95% of their orexin (hypocretin) neurons. A good example of how a careful look at brain tissue can reveal the cause of a disease.
