#### Question 1

When an action potential reaches the axon terminal, the entry of which ion directly triggers synaptic vesicles to release neurotransmitter?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Sodium (Na⁺)
2. Potassium (K⁺)
3. Calcium (Ca²⁺)
4. Chloride (Cl⁻)
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Depolarization of the terminal opens voltage-gated calcium channels, and the incoming calcium makes calcium-sensitive proteins pull vesicles into the membrane so they release their contents. Sodium (A) is the ion that drives the action potential along the axon, which is why it is a tempting choice, but it is not the release trigger.

    **Concept Tested:** Synaptic Vesicle
    **Bloom's Level:** Remember

#### Question 2

Which neurotransmitter is the main inhibitory neurotransmitter in the brain?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. GABA
2. Glutamate
3. Dopamine
4. Acetylcholine
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. GABA is used at most fast inhibitory synapses in the brain, and many sedative drugs work by boosting its effects. Glutamate (B) is the opposite: the main excitatory neurotransmitter. Dopamine and acetylcholine can have different effects depending on the receptor, but neither is the brain's main inhibitory transmitter.

    **Concept Tested:** GABA
    **Bloom's Level:** Remember

#### Question 3

At the neuromuscular junction, which neurotransmitter and receptor carry the signal from a motor neuron to a skeletal muscle fiber?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Norepinephrine acting on metabotropic receptors
2. Glutamate acting on ionotropic receptors
3. Acetylcholine acting on muscarinic receptors
4. Acetylcholine acting on nicotinic receptors
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Motor neurons release acetylcholine, which opens nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, a fast ionotropic type, on the muscle fiber. Muscarinic receptors (C) also respond to acetylcholine, but they are the slower metabotropic type found in places such as the heart, not on skeletal muscle.

    **Concept Tested:** Neuromuscular Junction
    **Bloom's Level:** Remember

#### Question 4

Which statement best describes the difference between an ionotropic receptor and a metabotropic receptor?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Ionotropic receptors bind neurotransmitters; metabotropic receptors bind only hormones.
2. An ionotropic receptor is itself an ion channel; a metabotropic receptor acts through a G protein and second messengers.
3. Ionotropic receptors are always inhibitory; metabotropic receptors are always excitatory.
4. Ionotropic receptors are found on the presynaptic cell; metabotropic receptors are found on the postsynaptic cell.
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. An ionotropic receptor opens a pore through itself when the neurotransmitter binds, giving a fast, brief effect. A metabotropic receptor has no pore and works by switching on a G protein, which triggers slower but longer-lasting changes. Option C is a common mix-up: whether a receptor excites or inhibits depends on which ions or pathways it affects, not on which family it belongs to.

    **Concept Tested:** Metabotropic Receptor
    **Bloom's Level:** Understand

#### Question 5

Electrical synapses are faster than chemical synapses. Why does the human brain still rely mostly on chemical synapses?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Chemical synapses can be adjusted: they can make a signal stronger or weaker, or turn it from excitatory to inhibitory.
2. Chemical synapses let ions flow directly between the two cells, which is more reliable.
3. Chemical synapses work in both directions, so information can flow backward.
4. Chemical synapses do not need the action potential to reach the axon terminal.
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The chemical step gives many control points (how much is released, which receptors respond, how fast it is cleared), which allows flexible signaling and learning. Options B and C describe electrical synapses, not chemical ones. Option D is wrong because release at a chemical synapse depends on the action potential arriving and opening calcium channels.

    **Concept Tested:** Chemical Synapse
    **Bloom's Level:** Understand

#### Question 6

What does an inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP) do to the postsynaptic neuron?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It triggers a smaller-than-normal action potential.
2. It moves the membrane potential away from threshold, making the neuron less likely to fire.
3. It blocks the presynaptic neuron from releasing neurotransmitter.
4. It speeds up reuptake so the synapse is cleared faster.
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. An IPSP hyperpolarizes the membrane or holds it down, often by letting chloride in or potassium out, so it subtracts from the total that reaches the axon hillock. Option A reflects a misconception: action potentials are all-or-none, so there is no "smaller" action potential. An IPSP changes the odds of firing, not the size of the spike.

    **Concept Tested:** Inhibitory Postsynaptic Pot.
    **Bloom's Level:** Understand

#### Question 7

A doctor prescribes a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) for a patient with depression. What is the most direct effect of the drug at a serotonin synapse?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It makes the presynaptic neuron produce much more serotonin.
2. It binds serotonin receptors and activates them in place of serotonin.
3. It keeps released serotonin in the synaptic cleft longer by blocking the serotonin transporter.
4. It breaks down serotonin in the cleft so receptors are not overstimulated.
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. SSRIs block the transporter that pumps serotonin back into the presynaptic terminal, so serotonin lingers in the cleft. Option B describes an agonist, which is a different way a drug can act. In the Synapse Simulator, the "Reuptake blocker" condition shows this effect: the cleft graph stays higher for longer.

    **Concept Tested:** Reuptake
    **Bloom's Level:** Apply

#### Question 8

A neuron rests at −70 mV, and its threshold is −55 mV. At the same moment, it receives an EPSP of +7 mV, an EPSP of +6 mV, and an IPSP of −4 mV. What happens?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It fires, because two EPSPs always outweigh one IPSP.
2. It fires, because the membrane reaches −57 mV.
3. It does not fire, because any IPSP cancels all EPSPs.
4. It does not fire, because the membrane reaches only −61 mV.
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Adding the inputs gives \( -70 + 7 + 6 - 4 = -61 \) mV, which is below the −55 mV threshold, so no action potential starts. Option B uses the wrong arithmetic, and even −57 mV would still be below threshold. Option C is wrong because integration is a sum: one IPSP subtracts a little, it does not erase everything.

    **Concept Tested:** Synaptic Integration
    **Bloom's Level:** Apply

#### Question 9

A 35-year-old woman has drooping eyelids and double vision that get worse through the day, and her blood test shows antibodies against acetylcholine receptors. Her doctor prescribes pyridostigmine, an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor. Why does this help?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Acetylcholine is broken down more slowly, so it lasts longer and has more chances to bind the receptors that remain.
2. It destroys the antibodies that are attacking the acetylcholine receptors.
3. It makes the motor neuron fire action potentials at a higher rate.
4. It acts as an antagonist that protects receptors from the antibodies.
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. In myasthenia gravis, antibodies reduce the number of working acetylcholine receptors. Slowing acetylcholinesterase keeps acetylcholine in the cleft longer, which helps the remaining receptors produce a large enough response to reach threshold. Option B confuses symptom treatment with immune therapy: pyridostigmine does not remove antibodies (treatments such as plasma exchange and FcRn blockers lower antibody levels instead).

    **Concept Tested:** Myasthenia Gravis
    **Bloom's Level:** Analyze

#### Question 10

In an experiment, a new drug leaves the amount and timing of neurotransmitter in the synaptic cleft completely normal, but the postsynaptic response becomes much smaller. Where is the drug most likely acting?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. On the presynaptic calcium channels, reducing release
2. On the postsynaptic receptors, as an antagonist
3. On the reuptake transporters, blocking them
4. On the synaptic vesicles, emptying them early
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. If the cleft concentration is normal, release and cleanup are working normally, so the problem must be in detecting the transmitter: an antagonist on the receptors fits. Options A and D would lower the amount of transmitter in the cleft, and option C would make it linger longer, so each of those would change the cleft measurement.

    **Concept Tested:** Agonist and Antagonist
    **Bloom's Level:** Analyze
