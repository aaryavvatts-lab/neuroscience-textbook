    Chapter 13: The Aging Brain and Neurodegeneration¶

    Summary¶

    Every brain changes with age, but dementia is not a normal part of aging. This chapter separates normal brain aging from neurodegeneration, the slow death of neurons that causes Alzheimer's disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and Huntington's disease. You will see how misfolded proteins clump into plaques and tangles, how biomarkers reveal disease years before symptoms, and what new medicines (anti-amyloid antibodies, antisense drugs, and gene therapy) can and cannot do.

    Brains that last a lifetime

    I'm a sea slug, so I don't live long enough to worry about getting old. You will, and so will everyone you love. This chapter is about telling ordinary forgetfulness apart from disease, and about some of the newest medicines in neurology. By the end, you'll be able to read a headline about an "Alzheimer's breakthrough" and judge what it really means.

    13.1 Is Forgetting My Name Normal?¶

    Maya is home from soccer practice, studying Brain Bee flashcards at the kitchen table, when her grandfather comes to visit. He hugs her and says, "Good to see you, Priya." Priya is Maya's mom. He laughs it off. But later he asks Maya the same question about her team three times in one hour, and her mom mentions that he recently got lost driving to a grocery store he has used for twenty years.

    Is this just what happens when people get old, or is something wrong with his brain? Families ask doctors this every day, and the answer can change a person's care and safety. To answer it, start with what a healthy aging brain looks like.

    13.2 Normal Brain Aging¶

    Think of your brain as a library that has been open for 80 years. The books are still there, but the librarians walk slower and finding a book takes longer. Almost everything still works. It just takes more time.

    That's normal brain aging: the gradual, expected changes in brain structure and thinking that happen as healthy people grow older, without disease. According to the National Institute on Aging (NIA), as people age:

    • certain brain regions shrink, including some that are important for learning and complex thinking
    • communication between neurons in some regions becomes less efficient
    • blood flow in the brain may decrease
    • inflammation may increase

    These changes show up as slower thinking. An older adult may take longer to find a word or recall a name, find multitasking harder, or have small drops in attention. The good news is that older adults often have larger vocabularies than younger adults, and given enough time, they usually learn new tasks just as well.

    The healthy aging brain shrinks somewhat, but it does not lose neurons in large numbers. That makes it very different from the diseases in the rest of this chapter.

    Normal forgetting versus dementia¶

    To tell normal aging from disease, doctors ask whether the memory or thinking problem interferes with everyday life. Forgetting a word now and then is normal. Getting lost on a familiar route, as Maya's grandfather did, is a warning sign. The table below sums up comparisons the NIA uses with families.

    Normal aging Possible sign of dementia
    Making a bad decision once in a while Making poor judgments much of the time
    Missing one monthly payment Having trouble managing bills at all
    Forgetting which day it is, then remembering later Losing track of the date or the season
    Sometimes forgetting which word to use Trouble following or having a conversation
    Losing things from time to time Misplacing things often and being unable to find them

    Memory problems can also come from treatable causes, such as depression, thyroid problems, low vitamin B12, medication side effects, or poor sleep. That's one reason to see a doctor instead of assuming it's "just age."

    13.3 When Neurons Die: Neurodegeneration and Protein Misfolding¶

    Neurodegeneration is the progressive loss of the structure and function of neurons, ending in neuron death. "Progressive" means it keeps getting worse over time. Most neurons are never replaced. A dead skin cell is swapped for a new one, but a neuron that dies in your hippocampus or spinal cord is usually gone for good.

    An origami problem¶

    A protein is like a strip of paper folded into an exact origami shape. The shape is everything: a crane can "fly," but a crumpled ball of the same paper can't do anything. Cells fold every protein carefully into its working shape.

    Protein misfolding is when a protein folds into the wrong shape. Misfolded proteins tend to be "sticky." They cling to each other and form clumps that cells struggle to clear, and over time the clumps damage the cell. Many very different brain diseases share this problem, as the table below shows. Each one has its own protein, its own target neurons, and its own symptoms.

    Disease Misfolded or toxic protein Main neurons or region hit First big symptoms
    Alzheimer's disease Beta-amyloid (outside cells) and tau (inside cells) Hippocampus and entorhinal cortex first, then cortex Memory loss
    Parkinson's disease (Chapter 7) Alpha-synuclein (Lewy bodies) Dopamine neurons of the substantia nigra Slow movement, tremor
    ALS (SOD1 type) Misfolded SOD1 Motor neurons Weakness, twitching
    Huntington's disease Abnormally long huntingtin protein, cut into toxic fragments Striatum (part of the basal ganglia) Uncontrolled movements, mood changes

    Same mistake, different neighborhoods

    The big idea of this chapter: many neurodegenerative diseases are "the wrong protein, piling up in the wrong place." Where the pile builds up decides whether a person first loses memory, movement, or muscle strength.

    13.4 Dementia and Alzheimer's Disease¶

    Dementia is not one disease. It is a general term for a loss of thinking, remembering, and reasoning severe enough to interfere with a person's daily life and activities (NIA). Some people with dementia also have trouble controlling emotions, or their personality changes. Dementia becomes more common with age: the NIA estimates that about one-third of people age 85 or older may have some form of it. But dementia is not a normal part of aging, and many people live into their 90s and beyond without any signs of it.

    Dementia has many causes, including vascular dementia (from damaged blood vessels; see Chapter 14), Lewy body dementia, and frontotemporal dementia. The most common cause in older adults is Alzheimer's disease: a progressive brain disease that slowly destroys memory and thinking skills and, eventually, the ability to carry out simple tasks. According to the NIA fact sheet, estimates vary, but more than 6 million Americans, most of them 65 or older, may have Alzheimer's disease.

    A doctor, a microscope, and 1906¶

    The disease is named after Dr. Alois Alzheimer. In 1906, he studied the brain of a woman who had died of an unusual mental illness that included memory loss, language problems, and unpredictable behavior. Under his microscope he saw abnormal clumps between cells and tangled bundles of fibers inside cells. Today we call them amyloid plaques and tau tangles, and more than a century later they are still the defining features of the disease.

    Where Alzheimer's starts¶

    Alzheimer's disease usually damages the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus first (NIA). You met the hippocampus in Chapter 9 as the structure that turns new experiences into lasting memories. That explains the classic first symptom: trouble forming new memories. A person may recall their wedding day but not that they already asked you a question. Later, the damage spreads to areas of the cortex used for language, reasoning, and social behavior. In the final stage, the brain has shrunk a great deal and the person depends on others for all care.

    Many people pass through an in-between stage first. In mild cognitive impairment (MCI), a person has more memory or thinking problems than is normal for their age but can still manage daily life. People with MCI have a higher risk of developing Alzheimer's disease, but not all of them do, and some even return to normal thinking.

    13.5 Plaques and Tangles: Inside the Alzheimer's Brain¶

    Amyloid plaques: trash piling up outside the cell¶

    Brain cells make a larger protein called amyloid precursor protein. When it is cut up, one of the small pieces is beta-amyloid. One form, beta-amyloid 42, is thought to be especially toxic (NIA). In Alzheimer's disease, too much beta-amyloid clumps together between neurons. An amyloid plaque is one of these dense clumps of misfolded beta-amyloid outside cells. Plaques sit among synapses and disrupt how neurons function.

    Tau tangles: a collapsed railroad inside the cell¶

    Every neuron has a transport system. Long tubes called microtubules work like railroad tracks, carrying nutrients and other cargo from the cell body down the axon (the long fiber you met in Chapter 1). A protein called tau acts like the railroad ties that hold the tracks steady.

    In Alzheimer's disease, chemical changes make tau let go of the microtubules. The loose tau sticks to other tau, forming threads that join into neurofibrillary tangles. A tau tangle is one of these twisted clumps of abnormal tau inside a neuron. With the tracks falling apart, cargo can't reach the synapses, and communication between neurons breaks down. Research summarized by the NIA suggests that when beta-amyloid builds up to a kind of tipping point, abnormal tau spreads rapidly through the brain.

    The rest of the damage¶

    The NIA describes other processes that add to the damage:

    • Synapse loss. Losing synapses (Chapter 3) is one of the earliest signs linked to cognitive decline.
    • Glia that stop helping. Microglia normally clear waste, including beta-amyloid. In Alzheimer's disease they may fail at this and instead release chemicals that cause long-lasting inflammation.
    • Blood vessel problems. High blood pressure, diabetes, and small strokes can reduce blood flow and add to the damage.
    • Brain shrinkage. As neurons die, regions shrink, a process called brain atrophy.

    Genes and Alzheimer's disease¶

    Most Alzheimer's disease appears after age 65 and doesn't follow a simple inheritance pattern. A small number of cases begin much earlier, between a person's 30s and mid-60s. According to MedlinePlus Genetics, some early-onset cases are caused by variants in the APP, PSEN1, or PSEN2 genes. These variants lead to extra beta-amyloid, and they are passed down in an autosomal dominant pattern, meaning one altered copy of the gene is enough to cause the disease.

    For the common late-onset form, the best-studied gene is APOE. A version called APOE e4 raises a person's risk, and two copies raise it more. But APOE e4 is a risk factor, not a sentence. Some people with Alzheimer's disease don't carry it, and many carriers never develop the disease.

    13.6 Biomarkers: Seeing Disease Before Symptoms¶

    A biomarker is something measured in the body that signals a normal process, a disease process, or the body's response to a treatment. Blood sugar, for example, is a biomarker for diabetes. Brain biomarkers let doctors "see" inside the brain without opening the skull.

    For a long time, the only sure way to diagnose Alzheimer's disease was to look at brain tissue after death. Now doctors can detect its biomarkers in living people:

    • Amyloid PET scan. A small amount of a radioactive tracer that sticks to amyloid is injected into a vein. A PET scanner then shows where amyloid has built up.
    • Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) tests. A sample of the fluid around the brain and spinal cord, taken with a needle in the lower back (a lumbar puncture), can show amyloid and tau levels.
    • Blood tests. On May 16, 2025, the FDA cleared the first blood test used to help diagnose Alzheimer's disease. It measures the ratio of two proteins in blood (a form of tau called pTau217, and beta-amyloid 42) to help detect amyloid plaques. It is cleared for adults 55 and older who already have symptoms, and results must be read along with other clinical information.

    Why timing is everything¶

    Biomarker research showed that the brain changes start long before the symptoms. The NIA says changes may begin a decade or more before symptoms appear.

    In 2010, Clifford Jack and colleagues proposed a widely cited hypothetical model of Alzheimer's biomarkers. In their model, amyloid biomarkers turn abnormal first, while the person still thinks normally. After a delay that differs from person to person, markers of neuron injury (such as tau and brain shrinkage on MRI) turn abnormal. Symptoms come last. The authors estimated that in people who later develop dementia, amyloid buildup might begin as much as two decades before symptoms. They also noted that roughly 20 to 40 percent of older adults with normal thinking already have significant amyloid in their brains.

    This adds a new stage of disease. Preclinical Alzheimer's disease is the stage when biomarkers show Alzheimer's brain changes but the person has no symptoms yet. The stages with symptoms are mild cognitive impairment and dementia. The MicroSim below lets you watch these stages unfold.

    13.7 Anti-Amyloid Antibodies: Treatments That Target the Disease¶

    For decades, Alzheimer's medicines could only treat symptoms. Cholinesterase inhibitors (such as donepezil) slow the breakdown of acetylcholine, a neurotransmitter important for memory, and memantine acts on glutamate signaling (NIA). They can help for a while but don't change what's happening to the neurons.

    How an antibody can clean up plaques¶

    Your immune system makes antibodies, Y-shaped proteins that latch onto a specific target and mark it for removal. An anti-amyloid antibody is a lab-made antibody, given by infusion into a vein (and for lecanemab, now also by injection under the skin), that binds to beta-amyloid and helps the brain's immune cells clear plaques away. It's like putting bright "pick me up" tags on trash so the cleanup crew (the microglia) finds it.

    Two anti-amyloid antibodies are FDA-approved and available as of this writing. (An earlier one, aducanumab, received accelerated approval in 2021, but its maker discontinued it in 2024.)

    Drug FDA status Key trial Who it's approved for
    Lecanemab (Leqembi) Accelerated approval January 6, 2023; converted to traditional (full) approval July 6, 2023. An under-the-skin autoinjector (Leqembi Iqlik) was approved for maintenance doses in August 2025 and for starting treatment in July 2026 Clarity AD, 1,795 participants, 18 months Mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to Alzheimer's, with confirmed amyloid
    Donanemab (Kisunla) FDA approval July 2, 2024 TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2, 1,736 participants, 76 weeks Mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to Alzheimer's, with confirmed amyloid

    Sources: FDA announcements for lecanemab (with the current Leqembi prescribing information for the under-the-skin form) and donanemab; the NIA describes both as fully approved.

    What the trials showed: real, but modest¶

    Both drugs removed a lot of amyloid and slowed decline on memory and daily-living rating scales compared with placebo. But "slowed" is the key word.

    In the lecanemab trial, people's scores on an 18-point dementia rating scale (where higher means more impaired) got worse by 1.21 points over 18 months on the drug and 1.66 points on placebo. That's a difference of 0.45 points, or about 27 percent less worsening than on placebo. The researchers called it "moderately less decline." The donanemab trial showed a slowing of similar size. People on these drugs still got worse, just more slowly. Neither drug stops the disease, reverses it, or brings back lost memories.

    That fits the MicroSim. By the time symptoms appear, tau has spread and many neurons have died. Removing amyloid at that point is like clearing debris after a fire: it may slow further damage, but it doesn't rebuild the rooms.

    The main risk: ARIA¶

    The most important side effect has a long name: amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA). ARIA shows up on MRI scans as temporary swelling in the brain, sometimes with small spots of bleeding. Usually it causes no symptoms. When it does, they can include headache, confusion, dizziness, vision changes, or nausea. Rarely, it is serious or life-threatening (FDA). Both drugs carry a boxed warning, the FDA's strongest label warning, about ARIA.

    In the trials, ARIA with swelling showed up on scans in 12.6 percent of people taking lecanemab and 24.0 percent of people taking donanemab. (The two trials enrolled somewhat different groups, so these numbers are not a head-to-head comparison.) People with two copies of APOE e4 have a higher risk of ARIA, so the FDA labels say to test for APOE e4 before starting treatment. Patients get regular MRI scans to watch for ARIA.

    Approved does not mean cure

    Watch out for two opposite mistakes. "There's nothing doctors can do for Alzheimer's" is wrong, but so is "the new drugs cure it." The honest version: anti-amyloid antibodies modestly slow decline in early-stage disease, they carry real risks, and they aren't for everyone.

    Can treatment start even earlier?¶

    If amyloid builds up for years before symptoms, would treatment in the preclinical stage work better? Large clinical trials are testing this in people who have amyloid but no symptoms, such as the AHEAD 3-45 study of lecanemab. Until those results are in, treating people without symptoms remains experimental.

    13.8 Cognitive Reserve: Why Some Brains Hold Up Better¶

    Two people can have similar plaques and tangles, yet one has dementia and the other thinks clearly. How?

    Picture two cities hit by the same storm, which knocks out some roads. A city with one road to the hospital is in trouble. A city with many side streets reroutes traffic, and most people never notice. Cognitive reserve is the brain's ability to cope with damage by using its networks more efficiently or by switching to backup routes, so that thinking stays normal longer.

    Neuropsychologist Yaakov Stern separates two related kinds of reserve in his 2012 review:

    • Brain reserve: differences in the brain's physical structure, such as size or number of neurons and synapses, that let it tolerate more damage.
    • Cognitive reserve: differences in how the brain uses its networks, built up through life experiences such as education, challenging work, and mentally stimulating activities.

    Reserve doesn't stop plaques from forming. It changes how much damage the brain can absorb before symptoms show. Jack and colleagues suggested this may help explain why the delay between amyloid and symptoms varies so much.

    Can you build reserve? In one NIA-funded study of almost 3,000 older adults, people who practiced four or five healthy behaviors (physical activity, not smoking, not drinking heavily, a Mediterranean-style diet, and mentally stimulating activities) had a 60 percent lower risk of Alzheimer's disease than people who practiced one or none (NIA). This was an observational study, so it can't prove the habits caused the lower risk. The NIA also notes that high blood pressure in middle age is linked to higher dementia risk, so protecting your heart may help protect your brain.

    Reserve starts now

    Cognitive reserve is built over a whole lifetime, and school counts. Learning hard things, staying active, sleeping well (Chapter 10), and keeping up friendships are investments your 80-year-old self will thank you for.

    13.9 Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS)¶

    Now we move from memory to movement. In Chapter 7, you learned that movement commands pass through two kinds of motor neurons. Upper motor neurons in the motor cortex send signals down to lower motor neurons in the brainstem and spinal cord, which connect to muscles.

    Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive neurodegenerative disease that kills motor neurons, the nerve cells that control voluntary movement and breathing. In the classic form, both upper and lower motor neurons die. ALS is also called Lou Gehrig's disease, after a famous baseball player who had it.

    What ALS looks like¶

    According to the NINDS, early symptoms include muscle twitches, cramps, stiffness, weakness in an arm, leg, or the neck, and slurred speech or trouble chewing and swallowing. Symptoms most often begin between ages 55 and 75. Over time, people lose the ability to walk, use their hands, speak, swallow, and breathe without help. Most people with ALS die of respiratory failure, usually within three to five years after symptoms begin, although about 1 in 10 people live 10 years or more.

    Most people with ALS can still reason and remember, so they are aware of what's happening. About 20 percent, however, also develop frontotemporal dementia, which affects personality, behavior, and language (MedlinePlus Genetics).

    The two kinds of motor neuron loss produce different signs, just as in Chapter 7 (NINDS):

    Damaged neurons Signs a doctor finds
    Lower motor neurons Weakness, muscle wasting (atrophy), twitches (fasciculations)
    Upper motor neurons Stiffness (spasticity), overactive reflexes

    Finding both sets of signs in the same patient is a big clue pointing toward ALS.

    Genes and ALS¶

    NINDS reports that nearly all ALS is sporadic, meaning it appears without a clear family history. About 10 percent of cases are familial, running in families. Mutations in a gene called C9orf72 cause about 25 to 40 percent of familial cases, and mutations in the SOD1 gene cause another 12 to 20 percent. The SOD1 gene normally makes an enzyme called superoxide dismutase, which breaks down harmful, charged oxygen molecules that cells make as by-products (MedlinePlus Genetics). Mutant SOD1 protein can misfold and clump inside motor neurons, and lowering it became one of the first goals of a genetic medicine for ALS.

    Treatment¶

    There is no cure. NINDS lists riluzole, which may reduce glutamate-related damage and extended survival by a few months in trials, and edaravone, which slowed functional decline in some people. Care teams also help with breathing, nutrition, speech devices, and mobility. For people with SOD1 mutations, a drug now targets the cause of their disease (Section 13.11).

    13.10 Huntington's Disease¶

    Huntington's disease is an inherited neurodegenerative disease that causes uncontrolled movements, emotional and psychiatric problems, and a decline in thinking ability. The adult form usually begins in a person's 30s or 40s (MedlinePlus Genetics).

    A genetic stutter¶

    Huntington's disease is caused by a change in the HTT gene, which makes a protein called huntingtin. Inside the gene is a short DNA "word" of three letters, C-A-G, repeated many times in a row like a stutter: CAG CAG CAG CAG...

    The number of repeats is what matters. MedlinePlus Genetics gives these ranges.

    Number of CAG repeats What it means
    10 to 35 Does not cause Huntington's disease (but 27 to 35 repeats can lengthen into the disease range when passed to a child)
    36 to 39 May or may not develop Huntington's disease
    40 or more Almost always develop Huntington's disease

    Too many repeats produce an abnormally long huntingtin protein. It is cut into toxic fragments that clump inside neurons, another example of protein misfolding. The neurons hit hardest are in the striatum, part of the basal ganglia (Chapter 7), which act like a brake on unwanted movements. When striatum neurons die, the brake fails, and the person develops chorea: jerky, dance-like movements they can't control. Depression, irritability, and trouble with planning and judgment are also common and can be among the earliest signs.

    Inheritance: a coin flip¶

    Huntington's disease is autosomal dominant: one altered copy of the gene is enough to cause it. So each child of a parent with Huntington's disease has a 50 percent chance of inheriting the gene variant (NINDS). The repeat can also grow longer as it passes from one generation to the next. Longer repeats tend to cause earlier symptoms, a pattern called anticipation.

    A hard question, worth thinking about

    This part is heavy. A simple blood test can show whether a healthy 20-year-old carries the Huntington's gene, yet no treatment can stop the disease. Whether to take that test is a very personal choice, which is why genetic counselors help families decide.

    Treatment¶

    NINDS states that no treatment can stop or reverse Huntington's disease. Medicines such as tetrabenazine, deutetrabenazine, and valbenazine can reduce chorea, and other medicines help with depression and other psychiatric symptoms. Researchers are testing genetic approaches to lower the harmful huntingtin protein, including experimental gene therapies. In September 2026, the company developing one of them (AMT-130) asked the FDA to review it for accelerated approval, but as of this writing no gene therapy for Huntington's disease is FDA-approved.

    13.11 Fixing the Message: Antisense Drugs and Gene Therapy¶

    Alzheimer's antibodies attack a protein after it is made. Some newer medicines act earlier, on the gene's message. Recall the central idea from biology class.

    DNA (gene) → mRNA (a working copy of the instructions) → protein

    Think of DNA as a cookbook locked in the library (the nucleus). To cook, the cell photocopies one recipe (the mRNA) and carries it to the kitchen (the ribosomes), where the protein is made.

    Antisense oligonucleotides: a sticky note on the recipe¶

    An antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) is a short, lab-made strand of genetic letters designed to stick to one specific mRNA, the way one half of a zipper fits only its partner. Depending on its design, an ASO can tell the cell to destroy that mRNA, so less of a harmful protein is made. Or it can change how the mRNA is spliced (cut and joined), so more of a useful protein is made. It's like a sticky note on one recipe saying either "don't make this" or "make the full version."

    Two ASOs are landmarks in neurology. Both are injected into the fluid around the spinal cord by lumbar puncture (an intrathecal injection), so the drug reaches the brain and spinal cord directly.

    • Nusinersen (Spinraza) treats spinal muscular atrophy (SMA), an inherited disease in which children lose lower motor neurons because they lack enough of a protein called SMN. Nusinersen changes how the mRNA from a backup gene, SMN2, is spliced, so the cell makes more full-length SMN protein (Finkel et al., 2017). In December 2016, it became the first FDA-approved drug for SMA, showing that an ASO could change the course of a motor neuron disease.
    • Tofersen (Qalsody) treats ALS caused by SOD1 mutations. It sticks to SOD1 mRNA and lowers the production of SOD1 protein, including the toxic mutant form (FDA). It was the first approved treatment aimed at a genetic cause of ALS. Its story, told in the Bedside case below, shows how biomarkers now shape drug approval.

    Gene therapy: delivering a new recipe¶

    Gene therapy treats disease by modifying a person's genes. According to the FDA, it can work by replacing a disease-causing gene with a healthy copy, inactivating a gene that is not working properly, or introducing a new or modified gene. One common approach uses a harmless, emptied-out virus as a delivery truck that carries a working copy of a gene into cells.

    The first gene therapy for SMA, onasemnogene abeparvovec (Zolgensma), was approved by the FDA on May 24, 2019 for children under 2 years old. A single infusion into a vein delivers a working copy of the human SMN gene to motor neurons.

    At the Bedside: Two Patients, Two Misfolded Proteins¶

    Meet the patient

    Mr. Okafor is a 74-year-old retired accountant. Over two years, his wife has noticed that he repeats stories, misses appointments, and recently paid the same bill twice. He still drives, dresses himself, and plays chess with his grandson. He says, "My memory isn't what it was, but I'm fine." His wife is worried.

    What the doctor checks: The neurologist takes a history from both Mr. Okafor and his wife, because people with memory loss often underestimate it. Blood tests rule out low vitamin B12 and thyroid problems. On a memory test he can't recall a word list after a few minutes, even with hints, but attention and language are mostly normal. An MRI shows mild shrinkage of the hippocampus on both sides and no stroke or tumor. An amyloid PET scan is positive, showing amyloid plaques across the cortex.

    Where is the problem? (localization): Trouble storing new memories, with older memories and language still working, points to the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex in the medial temporal lobes. The shrinkage on MRI matches. Because he still manages most daily activities, his symptoms fit mild cognitive impairment or the edge of mild dementia, not normal aging.

    Diagnosis: Early Alzheimer's disease (mild cognitive impairment due to Alzheimer's disease).

    Why it happens: As the biomarker model predicts, beta-amyloid has probably built up for many years. Tau tangles have spread into his memory circuits, synapses have been lost, and hippocampal neurons have died.

    Treatment today: His doctor discusses an anti-amyloid antibody, since he is in the early stage these drugs are approved for and his amyloid is confirmed. Before starting, he would have APOE e4 genetic testing and a baseline MRI, then regular MRIs to watch for ARIA. The doctor is honest about the trade-off. In trials, these drugs modestly slowed decline (they did not stop or reverse it), they require regular infusions or injections, and ARIA can rarely be serious. A cholinesterase inhibitor may also help symptoms. Just as important are safety planning (including driving), blood pressure control, exercise, social activity, and caregiver support.

    What's new in research: On May 16, 2025, the FDA cleared the first blood test to help diagnose Alzheimer's disease, for adults 55 and older with symptoms. The FDA said this less invasive option could reduce reliance on PET scans and make diagnosis more accessible.

    Your turn: diagnose a variation

    A 47-year-old woman has had weakness in her right hand for six months. She now drops her keys and trips on her right foot. Her memory is fine. On exam, the muscles in her hand are thin and twitch under the skin, yet her reflexes are unusually brisk. Her father and an aunt both died of a "muscle disease" in their 50s. What do you suspect, and what new treatment might her doctors discuss?

    Answer: Weakness, muscle wasting, and twitching (lower motor neuron signs) together with brisk reflexes (upper motor neuron signs), plus a family history, suggest familial ALS. Genetic testing could look for a SOD1 mutation. If one is found, doctors may discuss tofersen (Qalsody). It received FDA accelerated approval on April 25, 2023, based on its ability to lower neurofilament light in blood. Neurofilament light is a blood biomarker of axon injury and neurodegeneration. Accelerated approval is an FDA pathway for serious diseases when a drug improves a biomarker "reasonably likely" to predict real benefit. Continued approval may depend on trials confirming that benefit.

    Be honest about the evidence: in the phase 3 VALOR trial, tofersen lowered SOD1 and neurofilament light, but it did not meet its primary clinical endpoint, the change in a function rating scale at 28 weeks (Miller et al., 2022). A 52-week follow-up hinted that starting earlier was better, but that was not the trial's main test. A confirmatory trial (ATLAS) is testing whether tofersen delays symptoms in SOD1 carriers who are still healthy. Tofersen is given by lumbar puncture, and procedure-related side effects are common.

    This is a fictional teaching case. It is not medical advice. If you or someone you know has symptoms like these, talk to a doctor.

    Common Misconceptions¶

    Myth: Dementia is a normal part of getting old.
    Reality: Some slowing of memory and thinking is normal with age, but dementia is not. Dementia is caused by diseases that damage the brain, and many people live into their 90s and beyond without it.

    Myth: There are no treatments at all for Alzheimer's disease.
    Reality: Several medicines help with symptoms, and two anti-amyloid antibodies (lecanemab and donanemab) are FDA-approved for early-stage disease. They modestly slow decline but do not cure the disease.

    Myth: Removing amyloid plaques will bring back lost memories.
    Reality: By the time symptoms appear, tau has spread and many neurons have died. Neurons that have died are not replaced, so clearing amyloid can slow decline but cannot restore what was lost.

    Myth: If you carry the APOE e4 gene, you will definitely get Alzheimer's disease.
    Reality: APOE e4 raises risk but doesn't decide anyone's fate. Many carriers never develop the disease, and many people with Alzheimer's disease don't carry it. Huntington's disease is different: with 40 or more CAG repeats, a person almost always develops it.

    Brain Bee Practice¶

    1. Name the two abnormal protein deposits that define Alzheimer's disease, and say where each is found.

    Amyloid plaques, made of beta-amyloid, are found outside neurons. Neurofibrillary (tau) tangles, made of abnormal tau, are found inside neurons.

    2. Which two brain structures does Alzheimer's disease usually damage first, and what symptom does that explain?

    The entorhinal cortex and the hippocampus. Damage there explains early trouble forming new memories.

    3. What type of neurons die in ALS, and what are two signs of damage to each type?

    Motor neurons. Lower motor neuron damage causes weakness, muscle wasting, and twitching (fasciculations). Upper motor neuron damage causes stiffness (spasticity) and brisk reflexes.

    4. What genetic change causes Huntington's disease, and what are the chances that a child of an affected parent inherits it?

    An expanded CAG repeat in the HTT (huntingtin) gene. It is autosomal dominant, so each child has a 50 percent chance of inheriting it.

    5. What is an antisense oligonucleotide? Name one approved for a neurological disease.

    A short, synthetic strand of genetic material that binds a specific mRNA to reduce or change production of a protein. Examples: nusinersen for spinal muscular atrophy, or tofersen for SOD1-ALS.

    Exercises¶

    Remember¶

    1. Define neurodegeneration in your own words.

      Answer

      Neurodegeneration is the progressive loss of the structure and function of neurons, ending in their death.

    2. What is ARIA, and how do doctors check for it?

      Answer

      ARIA (amyloid-related imaging abnormalities) is temporary brain swelling, sometimes with small spots of bleeding, that can happen with anti-amyloid antibodies. Doctors check for it with regular MRI scans.

    Understand¶

    1. Explain why trouble forming new memories is often the first sign of Alzheimer's disease.

      Answer

      Alzheimer's damage usually begins in the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus, the structures that turn new experiences into lasting memories. Older memories stored elsewhere in the cortex are affected later.

    2. Explain the difference between a biomarker and a symptom, using Alzheimer's disease as an example.

      Answer

      A symptom is something the person or family notices, such as forgetting appointments. A biomarker is something measured in the body, such as amyloid on a PET scan or in blood. Biomarkers can become abnormal years before symptoms appear.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Normal aging brings slower thinking and some shrinkage, but not large-scale neuron loss. Dementia is not a normal part of aging.
    • Many neurodegenerative diseases share one theme: a misfolded protein builds up in specific neurons, and the location decides the symptoms.
    • Alzheimer's disease, the most common cause of dementia, is defined by amyloid plaques outside neurons and tau tangles inside them. It usually starts in the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex.
    • Biomarkers (PET scans, spinal fluid, and now an FDA-cleared blood test) show that Alzheimer's brain changes begin a decade or more before symptoms.
    • Anti-amyloid antibodies (lecanemab and donanemab) are FDA-approved for early Alzheimer's disease. They modestly slow decline, are not cures, and carry a risk of ARIA.
    • ALS kills upper and lower motor neurons. Huntington's disease comes from an expanded CAG repeat in HTT, is autosomal dominant, and damages the striatum.
    • Antisense oligonucleotides (nusinersen, tofersen) and gene therapy target genes and their messages. Tofersen's accelerated approval rested on a biomarker; its phase 3 trial missed its main clinical goal.
